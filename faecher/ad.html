Which information is the minimum needed to start the design of a new aircraft?

  • The payload mass mPL The designer cannot start without an absolute mass — every other mass in the sizing appears only as a fraction.
  • The number of passengers Drives the cabin length and therefore the whole fuselage layout.
  • The volume of the transport and system compartments Mass alone is not enough: low-density cargo can fill the hold long before the payload mass limit is reached.
  • The design mission The mission profile drives the fuel fraction and thus the take-off mass — this is what the Breguet analysis is applied to.
  • The wing reference area
  • The engine type and the number of engines

Which mass relation is the payload-range diagram based on, and what does the diagram show?

  • mTO = mOE + mF + mPL Take-off mass = operating empty mass + fuel mass + payload mass. Every point of the diagram is a different split of this sum.
  • It shows the transport capability of one specific aircraft: payload over range It describes an existing (or already sized) aircraft — unlike the seat-range diagram, which compares the whole market.
  • Beyond a certain range, payload has to be traded for fuel Once the tanks are full, more range is only possible by taking out payload, because mTO is limited.
  • It plots thrust-to-weight ratio over wing loading
  • It gives an overview of all aircraft in a market segment

How are payload factor and range flexibility defined, and what does experience show about them?

  • Payload factor = sold payload / available payload It corrects the average number of passengers on a route to a realistic seat capacity — assuming a permanently full aircraft is unrealistic.
  • Range flexibility = maximum range / average flight distance Airlines expect a margin: the maximum range must be clearly larger than the average leg actually flown.
  • Both rise with the range of the aircraft Long-range aircraft can be utilised better than short-range aircraft, and they carry more range reserve.
  • The realisable payload factor depends on the world economy and can change quickly A downturn hits the airlines first and, with a delay, the manufacturers.
  • The average flight distance may be used directly as the maximum range requirement

Which approaches are used for market studies of civil aircraft, and what is the "family" concept?

  • Analysis of the seat-range diagram, analysis of route concepts, and preparation of a market analysis The three approaches named in the lecture. The seat-range diagram reveals market niches, the route analysis delivers the reference mission, the market analysis the future demand.
  • A family consists of a basis aircraft plus stretched and shortened variants Example: A320 as the basis, stretched A321, shortened A319 and A318.
  • Between family members the number of seats should be altered by about 20 – 25 % Smaller steps do not justify a separate variant; larger steps leave gaps that the family no longer covers.
  • The basis aircraft is the design closest to the optimum The derivatives are compromises around it — which is why the basis version usually has the best economics.
  • Every gap found in the seat-range diagram is a market niche worth filling

Which EASA Certification Specification applies to which aircraft?

  • CS-25: Large Aeroplanes The specification behind almost every requirement used in the sizing of a transport aircraft — field length, climb gradients, seating rules.
  • CS-23: Normal, Utility, Aerobatic and Commuter Aeroplanes The general aviation counterpart to CS-25.
  • CS-22: Sailplanes and Powered Sailplanes Together with CS-VLA (Very Light Aeroplanes) and CS-LSA (Light Sport Aeroplanes) it covers the light end of the range.
  • CS-E covers engines and CS-P covers propellers Engines and propellers are certified separately from the airframe; CS-34 and CS-36 add emissions and noise.
  • CS-27 and CS-29 are the specifications for small and large aeroplanes

How does the general aircraft certification procedure work?

  • A Part-21J approved design organisation designs the aircraft and shows compliance with the applicable Certification Specifications, which leads to a type certificate The type certificate covers the design — one certificate for the whole type.
  • A Part-21G approved production organisation issues a certificate of airworthiness and a Statement of Conformity for each individual aircraft produced This is the step from "the design is approved" to "this particular aircraft matches the approved design".
  • Continued airworthiness has to be ensured according to Part-M, with maintenance done by a Part-145 organisation Plus a valid noise certificate and a valid insurance before the aircraft may be operated.
  • The head of design releases the prototype for the first flight The most important person in the design process — authorities delegate a large part of the certification duties to responsible persons inside the company.
  • One type certificate is sufficient to operate each individual aircraft of that type

Besides customer requirements and Certification Specifications, which constraints have to be considered?

  • Safety: fuel reserve, emergency exits, aisle width, ditching, oxygen supply, fire protection Many of these directly shape the fuselage and cabin layout of Chapter 7.
  • Airports and fleet strategy: gangways, ground equipment, turning radius, containers, fleet commonality An aircraft that does not fit the existing airport and fleet infrastructure is hard to sell — think of the gate constraints that fixed the A380 span at 80 m.
  • Structure and systems: loads and load factors, safe-life / fail-safe / damage tolerant philosophy, corrosion prevention, maintenance concepts, accessibility The construction philosophy is a design decision with consequences for weight and operating cost.
  • Flight performance: static and dynamic stability, trimming, controllability, control forces, stall behaviour, noise These are the constraints that later drive the empennage sizing in Chapter 11.
  • If the design complies with the Certification Specifications, no further constraints have to be considered

An untwisted wing is designed for a small propeller-driven general aviation aircraft. Which wing incidence angle is a reasonable starting value for conceptual design?

  • i ≈ 2° Typical conceptual-design values: home-builts and small propeller aircraft i ≈ 2°, jet transports i ≈ 1°, military jets i ≈ 0°.
  • i ≈ 1°
  • i ≈ 0°
  • i ≈ 8°

The incidence angle is chosen so that the wing sits at its design angle of attack while the fuselage sits at its minimum-drag attitude in cruise.

For a twisted wing the average incidence between root and tip should match these values.

A wing shall have a geometric twist of ε = −4° and an average incidence of 2°. Which root and tip incidence follow?

  • Root 4°, tip 0° Average = (4° + 0°)/2 = 2°, and the difference tip − root = −4° gives the required wash-out.
  • Root 2°, tip −2°
  • Root 3°, tip 1°
  • Root 6°, tip 2°

iavg = (iroot + itip)/2 and ε = itip − iroot.

For initial design purposes, which amount of geometric wash-out is normally assumed to provide adequate stall characteristics?

  • ε ≈ −3° About −3° of wash-out is the standard first guess: it unloads the tip so that the root stalls first.
  • ε ≈ +3°
  • ε ≈ −15°
  • ε = 0°

Twist optimised for one lift coefficient is only optimal at that condition — the more twist is applied, the worse the wing performs at other angles of attack.

The elliptical Spitfire wing also used about −2° of wash-out — the „perfect elliptical lift distribution“ story is a myth.

The airfoil of a high aspect ratio wing reaches cl,max = 1.6 in 2D. Which maximum lift coefficient should be assumed for the clean 3D wing in conceptual design?

Guideline: CL,max,wing = 0.85 … 0.92 · cl,max,airfoil

  • About 1.4 – 1.5 0.85 … 0.92 × 1.6 ≈ 1.36 … 1.47. The higher end of the band applies to high aspect ratio wings.
  • About 1.6, the 3D wing reaches the same value
  • About 1.8
  • About 1.0

Lower values within the band apply to low aspect ratio wings, higher values to high aspect ratio wings. Sweep reduces the value further, roughly with cos(Λc/4).

Which value is typically assumed for the dynamic pressure ratio ηH at the horizontal stabilizer?

  • ηH ≈ 0.90 The tail sits in the wake of wing and fuselage, so it sees a reduced dynamic pressure. The usual band is 0.85 … 0.95, with 0.90 as the standard assumption.
  • ηH = 1.00
  • ηH ≈ 0.50
  • ηH ≈ 1.20

Note the exception: on some small and multi-engine propeller aircraft the tail is placed intentionally in the slipstream to increase its effectiveness.

Which first guess is normally used for the distance of the total centre of gravity behind the leading edge of the mean aerodynamic chord (xCG,LEMAC)?

  • ≈ 0.30 · MAC A c.g. at roughly 30 % MAC is the standard starting assumption for placing the wing group relative to the fuselage group.
  • ≈ 0.25 · MAC
  • ≈ 0.75 · MAC
  • 0 · MAC (at LEMAC)

It is used in xLEMAC = xFG − xCG,LEMAC + (mWG/mFG)(xWG,LEMAC − xCG,LEMAC) to find the wing position.

Where is the aerodynamic centre of a lifting surface located?

  • At about 25 % of the chord in subsonic flow The classic quarter-chord position.
  • At about 40 … 50 % of the chord in supersonic flow The a.c. moves aft with supersonic flow — this rearward shift is a major source of trim drag for supersonic aircraft.
  • For a wing or stabilizer, on the mean aerodynamic chord at the same relative position as on the airfoil This is exactly why the MAC is constructed: it lets 2D airfoil knowledge be transferred to the 3D surface.
  • Always at the centre of gravity of the aircraft
  • It moves forward to about 10 % chord at supersonic speed

Careful: the formulas commonly used compute the mean geometric chord (MGC), which is only an approximation of the true MAC — the real MAC also depends on airfoil, twist and sweep.

Which aspect ratio band fits which aircraft category?

  • Low aspect ratio → fighters Low A gives high structural strength and high roll manoeuvrability — at the price of high induced drag.
  • Medium aspect ratio → general aviation and short-range transports A compromise between structural weight and drag.
  • High aspect ratio → long-range transports and sailplanes High A minimises induced drag and therefore maximises L/D — the decisive parameter for range and glide performance.
  • Very low aspect ratio → sailplanes

Remember the geometric coupling: for a fixed wing area, b = √(A · Sref) — a higher aspect ratio directly means a larger span.

Which quantities are plotted on the axes of a matching diagram (constraint diagram)?

  • Wing loading W/S on the horizontal axis W/S is the first of the two sizing outputs — it fixes the wing area once the take-off mass is known.
  • Thrust-to-weight ratio T/W (or power loading W/P) on the vertical axis The second sizing output — it fixes the required engine size.
  • Range over payload
  • Stabilizer area ratio over c.g. position

Each point performance requirement (take-off, landing, climb, cruise, ceiling, manoeuvre) is transformed into a curve in this plane. The feasible region is the intersection of all constraints; the design point is chosen inside it.

Which of the following requirements appear as an (almost) vertical line in the matching diagram?

  • Landing field length The approach/landing speed follows from W/S and CL,max,L only — thrust does not appear, so the constraint is a vertical limit on wing loading.
  • Maximum stall speed requirement Same reason: VS = √(2(W/S)/(ρ CL,max)) is independent of installed thrust.
  • Second-segment climb gradient with one engine inoperative
  • Take-off field length

Vertical constraints only limit W/S; horizontal constraints only limit T/W; all others couple both.

Landing normally sets the maximum wing loading, take-off and climb set the minimum thrust-to-weight ratio.

During wing design it turns out that the assumed CL,max,Landing cannot be achieved with the chosen high-lift system. What is the consequence for the sizing?

  • The landing constraint moves to a lower wing loading, so the wing has to become larger W/S at landing is proportional to CL,max,L. Less maximum lift means less permissible wing loading.
  • The preliminary sizing has to be repeated with the lower lift coefficients Exactly the feedback loop described in the lecture: sizing assumptions must be confirmed by the high-lift design, otherwise the loop is closed again.
  • The required take-off thrust may increase A reduced CL,max,T-O shifts the take-off constraint upwards, demanding more thrust for the same field length.
  • The wing can be made smaller

This is why Chapter 9 explicitly checks whether the high-lift system can deliver ΔCL,max = CL,max,T-O/L − CL,max,clean.

Breguet's range equation for a jet aircraft reads R = (V/c)·(L/D)·ln(W1/W2). Which measures increase the range?

  • Reducing the thrust specific fuel consumption c Range is inversely proportional to c — engine efficiency enters linearly.
  • Increasing the aerodynamic efficiency L/D L/D enters linearly, which is why drag reduction and high aspect ratios pay off directly in range.
  • Increasing the fuel mass fraction, i.e. the ratio W1/W2 More fuel relative to the landing weight increases the logarithm — but only logarithmically, so doubling the fuel does not double the range.
  • Increasing the operating empty weight at constant take-off weight
  • Increasing CD0

For a propeller aircraft the equivalent form is R = (ηP/cP) · (L/D) · ln(W1/W2) — the speed V drops out and the propeller efficiency takes its place.

Which flight condition gives the maximum range of a propeller-driven aircraft (Breguet, constant altitude)?

  • Flight at maximum L/D For a propeller the range equation contains L/D directly, so best range coincides with (L/D)max.
  • Flight at maximum CL3/2/CD
  • Flight at maximum CL1/2/CD
  • Flight at CL,max

Summary: propeller → best range at (L/D)max, best endurance at max CL3/2/CD. Jet → best range at max CL1/2/CD, best endurance at (L/D)max.

The designer increases the aspect ratio of the wing while keeping the wing area and the mission constant. Which effects on the sizing result are to be expected?

  • L/Dmax increases, so less fuel is needed for the same range Induced drag falls with 1/A, which raises the maximum aerodynamic efficiency.
  • The wing structural weight increases Larger span means larger bending moments and a heavier wing box — this counteracts the fuel saving.
  • A trade study is required to find the optimum, because the two effects work against each other This is the classical aspect ratio trade: aerodynamic gain versus structural penalty.
  • The lift curve slope CL,α decreases
  • The stall angle of attack increases

Effects of a reduced aspect ratio, for comparison: lower lift curve slope, higher stall angle of attack, lower CL,max, more induced drag, lighter structure.

Why is an accurate mass prediction so critical in the early design phase?

  • Mass directly drives aircraft size, engine thrust and therefore acquisition and operating cost The whole sizing loop is anchored on the take-off mass — an error propagates into every subsequent result.
  • Weight growth in late design stages severely degrades performance and is expensive to fix The Boeing 787 was about 8 % overweight in the early versions, which cost roughly 15 % of range.
  • The decision about the feasibility of a project is taken before the costly detail design starts A wrong mass estimate can lead to a project that is only recognised as unfeasible when large sums have already been spent — see the Boeing 2707 SST.
  • Mass distribution is irrelevant as long as the total mass is right

Component masses are needed to find the overall c.g., to place the wing and heavy systems, and as target values for the system designers.

What distinguishes Class I from Class II mass estimation methods?

  • Class I uses simple statistical fractions or factors derived from historical aircraft data Typically a component mass is expressed as a fixed percentage of MTOM or as a factor times a reference area.
  • Class II uses refined regression formulas including geometric and load parameters Class II formulas contain things like S, A, sweep, t/c, ultimate load factor and design speed.
  • Both are only valid for the aircraft class they were derived from Applying a transport-aircraft formula to a sailplane-like aspect ratio produces nonsense. The methods are not interchangeable.
  • Both methods are unit-independent
Always cross-check a mass estimate with a second method, and use real component masses whenever they are available.

Why is it desirable to place the centres of gravity of fuel, payload/systems and operating empty weight at the same longitudinal location?

  • It leads to a small c.g. travel If all three groups sit at the same station, burning fuel or changing the payload hardly moves the total c.g.
  • Less trim power is needed, so stabilizer areas and lever arms can be smaller The empennage has to cover the whole c.g. range — a narrow range means a small tail.
  • The reduced wetted area lowers the drag A smaller tail means less Swet and therefore a lower CD0 — the direct link from configuration decisions to aerodynamic performance.
  • It is sufficient if the condition holds for the full-fuel case
  • It allows a larger horizontal stabilizer, which improves stability

Why must the critical Mach number of stabilizer and control surfaces be higher than that of the wing?

  • Because the flow around the stabilizers must stay undisturbed even when the flow around the wing is already disturbed Control must be maintained in all situations — control surface deflections in separated flow are meaningless.
  • It is achieved through the choice of airfoil, thickness ratio and sweep angle of the stabilizer Rule of thumb for transonic aircraft: sweep about 5° higher and t/c about 10 % lower than the outboard wing, giving roughly ΔMacrit = 0.05.
  • The wing itself should be designed so that the aircraft does not cruise too far into the drag rise The second of the "always keep in mind" rules: airfoil type, t/c and sweep have to be chosen so that no excessive drag rise occurs at cruise Mach number.
  • A thicker stabilizer airfoil raises its critical Mach number
  • The requirement only concerns passenger comfort, not safety

Which further basic technical considerations govern the configuration layout?

  • Major components should intersect as close as possible to 90° to minimise interference drag Applies to nacelle on wing, nacelle on fuselage, wing on fuselage. Where 90° is impossible, fairings are needed.
  • At high subsonic speeds local area ruling should be applied For aircraft with supersonic requirements the cross-sectional area distribution must be made as smooth as possible — wave drag then becomes an essential design consideration.
  • Major structural components should be grouped to avoid duplicating heavy structure (structural synergism) Example: on a high-wing transport with fuselage-mounted gear, attach the landing gear to the same frames that carry the wing. The Piaggio P-180 shares wing box, aft pressure bulkhead and main gear structure.
  • Configuration design is also influenced by marketing, emotional and styling considerations Explicitly named in the lecture — and the same point returns for the vertical tail: sometimes the solution that performs worse but looks better is the right solution.
  • Accessibility and maintainability are detail-design concerns and can be ignored in the conceptual phase

Why are most aircraft built today of conventional (tail-aft) configuration?

  • In many cases there is no compelling reason for deviating, but there are reasons against deviating The burden of proof lies with the unconventional layout, not with the conventional one.
  • The database and experience for conventional configurations are very large All the statistical methods used throughout the design — Class I and II mass estimation, tail volume coefficients, Oswald factors — were derived from conventional aircraft.
  • For unconventional configurations the database is narrow or non-existent, which brings a very high risk of an unsuccessful design The Beechcraft Starship is the textbook example: about one ton overweight, 300 million dollars of development cost, only 53 aircraft built, and finally withdrawn from the market.
  • Because certification specifications forbid unconventional configurations
  • Because conventional configurations always have the best trimmed lift-to-drag ratio

Which statements about canard and tandem-wing configurations are correct?

  • They tend towards better trimmed lift-to-drag ratios The forward surface carries lift instead of producing the download of a conventional tail.
  • The canard must stall before the main wing Otherwise an uncontrollable pitch-up can develop — the canard losing lift first gives a nose-down recovery.
  • The canard may spoil the lift distribution of the main wing Its downwash hits the inboard main wing, which is exactly the region designed to stall first.
  • Maximum benefits are only possible with an unstable design Which means an artificial stability system — extra complexity, extra risk and extra certification effort.
  • To trim out the negative pitching moment of the main-wing flaps, the forward wing must be able to produce high lift Achieved with flaps on the canard, variable sweep or high incidence — one reason why canards rarely carry powerful main-wing flaps.
  • The main wing must stall before the canard

Which statements about delta wing configurations are correct?

  • The structure is very robust, which suits high load factors The deep root chord allows a very stiff, light wing box — one reason deltas dominate among fighters and fast bombers.
  • Good high-α and post-stall behaviour thanks to vortex lift The leading-edge vortices keep generating lift far beyond the conventional stall angle.
  • The large wing gives a low wing loading and high lateral manoeuvrability The reference area is enormous relative to the aircraft size.
  • The low lift-curve slope forces high angles of attack for maximum lift A problem at take-off and landing: long, nose-high approaches and a landing gear that has to allow large rotation angles.
  • The low aspect ratio produces high drag at high lift Induced drag scales with 1/A — the price for the robust, low-wave-drag planform.
  • A delta wing has particularly low induced drag in slow flight

Which statements about flying wing and blended wing-body configurations are correct?

  • No weight is spent on an empennage, and they tend towards higher trimmed L/D Together with the good payload weight fraction and mass distribution, these are the classic arguments for the layout.
  • Military versions benefit from a low radar cross section The Northrop B-2 is the standard example.
  • The usable c.g. range is very small Without a tail, the trim authority is limited, so the whole loading envelope must fit into a narrow band — a hard constraint on payload flexibility.
  • They tend towards low pitch damping, so a digital flight control system may be needed Longitudinal stability has to be provided artificially.
  • Pressurisation is problematic and the roll motion is unpleasant for passengers seated away from the centreline Which is why the concept is generally considered better suited to freight operations.
  • The flat cross section is structurally ideal for a pressurised cabin

Which statements about the three-surface configuration are correct?

  • It minimises induced trim drag over a wider range of c.g. positions With both a canard and a tail, the trim load can be shared instead of being carried by one surface alone.
  • Ideally the total lift of wing, canard and tail stays the same for all control inputs The surfaces work against each other rather than against the wing.
  • It costs increased complexity, additional weight and interference drag Three lifting surfaces mean three sets of structure, systems and junctions.
  • No aircraft has ever been built in this configuration
  • It reduces the zero-lift drag compared with a conventional layout

What distinguishes a "fixed size" from a "rubber size" engine in conceptual design?

  • Fixed size means an existing engine is chosen, so sizes and weights are known The COTS approach: the aircraft is designed around the engine. Low risk, but the engine is never a perfect match.
  • Rubber size means a new engine is assumed to be designed to fit the aircraft, so sizes and weights have to be estimated Gives the best performing aircraft, because the engine matches the requirements exactly.
  • Developing a new engine alongside a new aircraft can delay the whole aircraft project Rolls-Royce RB.211 and the L-1011 TriStar, EPI TP400 and the A400M, Pratt & Whitney F135 and the F-35 — all with massive financial penalties.
  • For a low risk design, choose an existing engine and design the aircraft around it The explicit recommendation of the lecture's lessons learned.
  • The rubber engine approach carries no risk, since the engine is designed to fit

On which considerations does the choice of the number of engines depend?

  • The ratio of the total thrust needed to the thrust available per engine The most basic constraint: if no single engine delivers enough thrust, more engines are needed.
  • Safety considerations, i.e. the probability of failure More engines mean a higher probability that one fails — but a smaller share of thrust lost when it does. That trade also drives the CS-25.121 climb gradients.
  • Economic considerations: drag, engine efficiency, weight of propulsion and fuel system, purchasing and operating cost Fewer, larger engines are usually more efficient — larger engines produce higher thrust per engine weight.
  • More engines are always the safer and therefore better choice
  • The number of engines has no influence on the matching diagram

Which statements about under-wing mounted jet engines are correct?

  • It is the most used configuration for transport aircraft Good accessibility for maintenance and bending moment relief for the wing structure.
  • The engine mass relieves the wing bending moment The engine weight acts opposite to the lift, reducing the root bending moment — a genuine structural weight saving.
  • FOD ingestion is a risk, so a clearance of roughly half a nacelle diameter to the ground is used as a rule of thumb A hard geometric constraint that couples the engine position to the landing gear length of Chapter 10.
  • Engine-out controllability suffers and the noise emission to the ground is high The lateral offset yE of a wing-mounted engine is exactly what sizes the vertical stabilizer for the OEI case.
  • The wing shields the engine noise from the ground

Match the jet engine installation to its characteristic advantage or drawback.

  • Over-wing: no FOD problem and the thrust axis lies close to the vertical c.g., but stronger pylons are required The small thrust-induced pitching moment is the main aerodynamic argument; the disturbed flow on the upper surface, where about two thirds of the lift is generated, is the main penalty.
  • Aft fuselage: a clean wing gives higher aerodynamic efficiency and the lateral spacing is small, but wing vortices can be ingested at high α The A-10 needs a fixed slat on the inner wing for exactly this reason.
  • Tail mounted: no FOD problem and the nacelle can be counted towards the vertical tail area, but the tail gets heavier and accessibility is poor Combined with an S-duct it can also reduce fuselage separation drag, as on the L-1011 — but the duct needs careful design to avoid internal separation.
  • Buried in the fuselage: less wetted area than a podded engine and a lower radar cross section, but the engines take up fuselage space A good choice for fighters, where afterburners are also integrated easily — and a poor one for large payloads.
  • Over-fuselage installation is fully compatible with a conventional tail

Which statements about tractor and pusher propeller installations are correct?

  • A fuselage tractor gives a short forebody and therefore a long tail lever arm Good stability with a small stabilizer — plus good cooling and a propeller in undisturbed air.
  • A fuselage pusher gives good visibility over the nose and less cabin noise Wing and fuselage also work in undisturbed air, which reduces skin friction drag and fuselage separation drag.
  • A pusher suffers from reduced propeller efficiency and needs a propeller clearance of at least 0.22 m during rotation and landing Which usually forces a longer, heavier landing gear. The short tail lever arm is the other main drawback.
  • A wing-mounted tractor gives bending moment relief and leaves the fuselage in undisturbed air At the price of engine-out controllability problems and long gear legs on low-wing designs.
  • On a wing-mounted tractor the crew should sit exactly in the propeller blade plane

What has to be watched when estimating the engine mass?

  • The weight quoted by the engine manufacturer is the non-integrated (dry) engine weight Pylon, cowling, intake, fuel system, propeller and thrust reverser still have to be added by the aircraft manufacturer.
  • Integration can increase the dry weight by a factor of about 1.3 – 1.7 Strongly dependent on the individual configuration — one of the classic sources of an optimistic mass estimate.
  • Roskam gives We = 0.213 · TTO for non-afterburning turbofans, in pounds The Cessna method We = Kp · PTO covers propeller aircraft up to 200 kts, with Kp = 1.1–1.8 for piston and 0.35–0.55 for turboprop engines.
  • For a two-engine small piston aircraft the integrated engine weight can reach about 30 % of the operating empty weight For large turbofan aircraft the fraction tends towards about 17 % — larger engines produce higher thrust per engine weight.
  • The engine position may be ignored in the c.g. estimation, as long as the total mass is right

Which orders of magnitude apply to the drag of the propulsion installation?

  • Nacelle (total engine) drag of a typical jet airliner: about 5 – 10 % of the total aircraft drag Only estimation factors can be given, because nacelle drag interacts strongly with the wing flow and depends heavily on the inlet geometry.
  • Total engine drag of a propeller aircraft: about 8 – 12 % of the total aircraft drag This figure typically already includes the cooling drag, which is significant for propeller-driven aircraft.
  • Cooling drag is partly caused by flow separation from the exhaust gases And the sizing of the cooling area is normally left to specialists — far too many variables for conceptual design.
  • An under-wing installation yields less drag, regardless of the engine type About two thirds of the lift is produced on the upper surface, so disturbing the lower surface costs less.
  • Nacelle drag can be predicted very accurately from the frontal area alone

Which statements about electric and hybrid electric propulsion are correct?

  • Efficiency and scalability make it possible to distribute the propulsion system over the whole aircraft And no heavy gearboxes are needed — the electric motor turns at the required speed directly.
  • Performance is independent of altitude, and efficiency is mostly independent of size Which is why the power lapse models of Chapter 5 (density ratio, Gagg and Ferrar) do not apply to electric aircraft.
  • Battery storage is highly inefficient compared with fossil fuels Motor gasoline reaches about 11 600 W·h/kg in practice, while even Lithium-Sulfur only reaches about 350 W·h/kg — a factor of more than 30.
  • New statistical mass estimation methods are needed and the sizing process has to be modified Plus very different cooling needs and therefore different drag.
  • A hybrid layout combines the advantages of both systems without a weight penalty

Which "lessons learned" does the lecture draw from the propulsion layout?

  • The propulsion layout must be right on the first attempt, or extensive rework follows Engines are typically the largest single items in an aircraft, both by weight and by size.
  • If you change the propulsion layout, you redesign the entire aircraft Which is why propulsion integration is the first step after preliminary sizing — no other system influences the design as much.
  • A rubber engine gives the best performing aircraft, but carries the risk of later changes in performance, mass, size and cost The choice between rubber and fixed engine is a risk decision, not a purely technical one.
  • Propulsion integration is a simple task that can safely be left to the end of the design

Which tasks does the fuselage have to fulfil?

  • Provide the space for crew, passengers and baggage, cargo, mission equipment and possibly fuel For a passenger aircraft this is the dominant driver: with a given number of passengers and seats abreast, length and diameter are essentially determined.
  • Carry the forces and moments coming from the empennage Which is why the tail cone structure grows with the stabilizer size — one more reason to keep the stabilizers small.
  • Carry the internal pressure of a pressurised cabin and the inertia forces from manoeuvres and landing The pressure load is what makes the circular cross section structurally attractive.
  • Contribute as little as possible to the overall drag The fuselage adds a large wetted area without producing lift — it is pure CD0.
  • Generate a significant share of the total lift

Which statements about the fuselage fineness ratio lF/dF are correct?

  • Minimum fuselage drag for subsonic aircraft is achieved at about lF/dF = 6 – 8 Shorter and thicker means more pressure drag; longer and thinner means more wetted area.
  • A longer fuselage increases the lever arm of the stabilizers and therefore allows smaller stabilizer areas So the drag optimum of the fuselage alone is not the optimum of the whole aircraft — less stabilizer area means less structural weight and less drag.
  • Stretch versions reach about lF/dF = 14, shrink versions as little as about 5 A family shares the cross section, so the fineness ratio is what changes.
  • For supersonic aircraft the minimum drag is achieved for lF/dF → ∞ Long and slender is what minimises wave drag — the same reasoning behind the Sears-Haack body and the area rule.
  • For a non-circular cross section the fineness ratio cannot be defined

  • About 190 m
  • About 16 m
  • About 5 m

The statistical factor kcabin = 1.0 … 1.1 m covers not only the seat pitch but also the lavatories, galleys, wardrobes, doors and emergency exits distributed along the cabin.

Which rule of CS 25.817 governs the number of seats abreast?

  • No more than three seats may be accessed from one aisle An evacuation requirement — nobody should have to climb over more than two seated passengers to reach an aisle.
  • Up to 6 seats across → single aisle (narrow body), maximum 3 – 3 seating Two rows of three, each with its own side of the aisle.
  • More than 6 and up to 12 seats across → twin aisle (wide body), maximum 3 – 6 – 3 seating In practice not more than 3 – 5 – 3 is used, on the A380.
  • An 8-abreast cabin may be laid out as 4 – 4 with a single aisle
  • The rule is only a comfort recommendation and may be exceeded

How is the required cargo compartment volume estimated?

  • From the baggage and cargo masses of the requirements, using densities of about 170 kg/m³ for baggage and 160 kg/m³ for cargo The requirements of Chapter 1 give masses; the cross section has to provide the corresponding volume.
  • The cargo compartment cross section should if possible be chosen so that standardised containers fit Container compatibility is an operational requirement of the airlines — an aircraft that needs its own container type is at a disadvantage.
  • Underfloor cargo compartments are usually used from about six seats per row upwards Below that the fuselage is simply not deep enough; smaller aircraft carry cargo in dedicated cabin space at the front and rear.
  • The fuselage cross section is derived from the cargo volume alone
  • If the payload mass fits, the payload volume automatically fits as well

Which statements about the fuselage cross section and the cockpit are correct?

  • For pressurised aeroplanes the circle is the structurally most efficient cross section Internal pressure produces pure membrane stress in a circular shell — any other shape needs bending-stiff frames.
  • For small aeroplanes a circular cross section is wasteful in terms of usable volume Hence the remark from Short Aircraft: we put square things in our aircraft — why should we make the fuselage a circle?
  • The cockpit window inclination should not be less than 30° to avoid reflections Flat windows are cheaper, curved windows give lower drag — a classic cost versus performance decision.
  • Over-nose and over-side vision angles are critical for safety, especially during take-off and landing In conceptual design it is enough to ensure the crew has the best possible view; the vision angles then fix the length and shape of the nose section.
  • The cross section is determined by the drag requirement alone

How is the fuselage of a general aviation aircraft structured?

  • Into three modules: engine bay, cockpit and passenger cabin, and tail lever arm Separated by a firewall at the engine bay and by the back of the cabin.
  • The only purpose of the aft part is to provide lever arm for the tail So it should be kept as simple as possible, with a smooth transition from the cabin to prevent flow separation.
  • Cockpit and passenger cabin are not separated; passengers sit directly behind the pilots The cabin and seats are drawn around a scaled drawing of a human — the sizing is driven by headroom and shoulder room requirements.
  • The baggage mass behind the passengers is limited by the c.g. travel A long lever arm behind the c.g. means even a modest baggage mass moves the c.g. a long way aft.
  • On a twin-engine aircraft with wing-mounted engines the nose must stay empty

What governs the layout of the nose and tail section of a transport aircraft?

  • The length of the nose section follows from the cockpit space and the required over-nose vision angle The cockpit must let the crew safely control and monitor the aircraft — in conceptual design, ensuring the best possible view is sufficient.
  • The inclination of the tail must be chosen so that no tail strike occurs at the maximum pitch angle during take-off The rotation angle is a hard geometric constraint linking fuselage, wing position and gear.
  • The final tail inclination can only be fixed after the landing gear has been designed Gear length and position determine the rotation point and therefore the achievable pitch angle — one of the feedback loops between Chapters 7 and 10.
  • The overall fuselage length is fixed at the very beginning and never changes

Which parameters still have to be determined during wing design, after preliminary sizing has already delivered the wing area?

  • Sweep angle and taper ratio Both are planform parameters fixed in Chapter 8.
  • Airfoil, twist and incidence angle The "wing design" group of parameters, as opposed to the planform group.
  • Dihedral angle and the location and size of aileron and spoiler Dihedral drives lateral stability; aileron size drives roll control power and constrains the space available for trailing-edge flaps.
  • The wing reference area Sref, which is not known before

The aspect ratio has usually been estimated already in preliminary sizing (via the assumed L/Dmax), but it is revisited and iterated in wing design.

Which statements about the effect of the aspect ratio A are correct?

  • Increasing A reduces the induced drag CD,i = CL²/(π A e) — the induced drag falls inversely with A.
  • Increasing A increases the wing structural weight The larger span raises the root bending moment.
  • Reducing A increases the stall angle of attack Low aspect ratio wings stall late and gently — this is why stabilizers are given low aspect ratios.
  • Reducing A reduces CL,max Together with the reduced lift curve slope this is one reason why low-A wings need large angles of attack.
  • Reducing A increases the lift curve slope

What are the two legitimate reasons to sweep a wing?

  • To delay the onset of shock waves and thus the wave drag rise Sweep reduces the effective Mach number normal to the quarter chord: Maeff = Ma · cos(Λc/4).
  • To fix a centre-of-gravity problem Classic example: the DC-3 used sweep to cure an aft-c.g. problem; canard or aft-engine layouts may also need it.
  • To improve the aerodynamic efficiency at low speed
  • To improve the stall behaviour of an aft-swept wing
  • To reduce the wing structural weight
Regarding the critical Mach number it makes no difference whether the sweep is positive or negative — only |cosΛ| matters.

A wing has a quarter-chord sweep of 30° and an unswept maximum lift coefficient of 1.5. Which value should be used as a first approximation for the swept wing?

  • ≈ 1.3 cL,max,swept ≈ cL,max,unswept · cos(Λc/4) = 1.5 · cos30° ≈ 1.30.
  • 1.5, unchanged
  • ≈ 1.7
  • ≈ 1.1

Sweep also reduces the lift curve slope and shifts CL,max to a higher angle of attack.

Why do aft-swept wings tend to "pitch up" at the stall?

  • The tip stalls first, and the tip is located behind the c.g. Losing lift behind the c.g. removes a nose-down contribution.
  • The still-attached inboard wing forward of the c.g. keeps lifting and produces a nose-up moment The result is a strong nose-up moment that drives the aircraft deeper into the stall — a self-aggravating and dangerous behaviour.
  • Forward-swept wings are completely free of pitch-up
  • Forward-swept wings lose aileron control first

Counter-measures on aft-swept wings: wash-out, spanwise change of airfoil (aerodynamic twist), fences, vortilons, and a careful choice of taper ratio.

Which effects does a decreasing taper ratio λ = ctip/croot have?

  • The structural weight can be reduced The centre of pressure shifts inboard, reducing the wing bending moments.
  • The available fuel volume is reduced The outboard wing becomes thin and small, so tank volume is lost.
  • The lift and lift-coefficient distribution over the span changes, affecting induced drag and stall behaviour Taper is the main planform tool to approach an elliptical lift distribution — but too small a taper ratio triggers tip stall.
  • There is no practical lower limit for the taper ratio

Additional effect: the low local Reynolds number at a small tip chord further promotes the tendency towards tip stall.

Which statements about the airfoil thickness ratio t/c are correct?

  • Wing structural weight varies approximately inversely with the square root of t/c Wwing ~ 1/√(t/c). Halving the thickness ratio therefore increases the wing weight by about 41 %.
  • In subsonic conditions the maximum lift is usually achieved around t/c = 12 … 16 % Both thinner and much thicker airfoils give lower cl,max.
  • A larger t/c lowers the critical Mach number A thicker airfoil accelerates the flow more strongly, so sonic conditions are reached at a lower free-stream Mach number.
  • t/c influences the available volume for structure, fuel and landing gear A practical constraint that often dominates over the pure aerodynamic optimum.
  • t/c has practically no influence on drag

Which stall tendency belongs to which airfoil thickness class?

  • t/c > 14 % ("fat"): trailing edge separation, gradual loss of lift, small change of pitching moment The gentle behaviour comes at a price: control power in the flapped section is lost early.
  • t/c = 6 … 13 % ("normal"): leading edge separation, sudden loss of lift and sudden change of pitching moment Control power is however maintained up to higher angles of attack.
  • t/c < 6 % ("thin"): leading edge separation, gradual loss of lift but significant change of pitching moment Control power is also maintained to high α.
  • t/c < 6 %: trailing edge separation with a very gentle stall

For wings with low or medium aspect ratio and/or high sweep, the stall behaviour is dominated by twist and sweep — in that case the 2D stall type can be ignored during airfoil selection.

What does the designation NACA 23012 mean?

  • 2 % camber at 30 % chord, 12 % thick
  • Laminar flow to 23 % chord, design Cl = 0.1, 2 % thick

The 5-digit series was designed for superior maximum lift and low pitching moments — but it has a harsh stall behaviour.

Match the airfoil designations to their meaning.

  • NACA 2414: 2 % camber at 40 % chord, 14 % thick 4-digit series — good stall behaviour, well suited for general aviation.
  • NACA 63-314: laminar flow over 30 % of the chord, design Cl = 0.3, 14 % thick 6-series — emphasis on laminar flow. The 2nd digit gives the minimum-pressure point in tens of percent.
  • SC(2)-0714: second-phase supercritical airfoil, design Cl = 0.7, 14 % thick NASA SC-series — good transonic behaviour while retaining decent low-speed characteristics.
  • NACA 651-412: the subscript 1 means 1 % thickness

An "A" after the second digit of a 6-series airfoil means the trailing edge cusp has been removed for easier manufacturing.

Which statements about wing twist are correct?

  • Geometric twist rotates the same airfoil section along the span αtip < αroot for wash-out.
  • Aerodynamic twist uses different airfoils along the span αzero-lift,tip < αzero-lift,root. In practice both types are usually combined.
  • Twist can be used to approach an elliptical lift distribution and thus minimum induced drag But only for the single angle of attack for which it was optimised.
  • The main purpose of wash-out is to prevent tip stall Unloading the tip makes the root stall first, which preserves aileron authority and gives a gentler stall.
  • A strongly twisted wing performs well at every angle of attack

Which measures produce the same effect as an increase of the geometric dihedral angle?

  • Moving the wing from a low to a mid or from a mid to a high position (≈ +3.5° each) Wing position contributes strongly to the effective dihedral — which is why low-wing aircraft need noticeably more geometric dihedral than high-wing aircraft.
  • Adding aft sweep (+10° of sweep ≈ +1° of dihedral) In sideslip the leading wing presents a smaller effective sweep and generates more lift.
  • Adding winglets or cranked wing tips Their primary purpose is vortex drag reduction, but they also add roll stability — sometimes used as a design fix.
  • Adding anhedral

Flight-mechanical consequence of positive effective dihedral: the spiral mode is stabilised, but the Dutch roll mode is destabilised.

How does a positive dihedral angle generate its stabilizing rolling moment?

  • The bank angle causes a sideslip, which increases the angle of attack of the lowered wing and thus its lift The aircraft "slides" towards the lowered wing; the resulting rolling moment tends to level the aircraft again.
  • The lowered wing has more effective area
  • It acts immediately, without any sideslip
  • The vertical tail is pushed sideways and yaws the aircraft level

Which flight situations size the ailerons?

  • Situations with low dynamic pressure, i.e. slow flight The rolling moment scales with q; the slowest required manoeuvre is therefore the critical one.
  • Situations requiring high roll accelerations about the longitudinal axis Typically crosswind landing corrections or, for military aircraft, manoeuvre requirements.
  • High-speed cruise at high altitude
  • Only the deflection angle matters, position and size are irrelevant

Important coupling: the span taken by the ailerons is not available for trailing-edge high-lift devices — and vice versa.

What are spoilers used for?

  • Reducing lift after touchdown or during a rejected take-off Dumping the lift puts weight on the wheels and makes the brakes effective — a major contribution to the braking distance.
  • Increasing drag as an air brake Used for descent and speed control.
  • Roll control by differential deflection Roll spoilers are common on transport jets to augment roll power, especially at low speed.
  • Increasing the maximum lift coefficient for landing

They sit on the upper surface, forward of the flaps and typically aft of the maximum thickness position. A first guess for the design is a position at about 60 % chord.

The design lift coefficient is the CL the aircraft flies at for most of its mission — the airfoil should have its best L/D exactly there, not just a high L/D somewhere else.

Which statements about high-wing versus low-wing configurations are correct?

  • A high wing gives good downward visibility and an easy, light wing attachment No carry-through structure through the cabin, and the wing box does not eat into the payload volume near the c.g.
  • A low wing shortens the required landing gear length The gear can be attached to the wing close to the ground — one reason why most transport jets are low-wing.
  • A mid wing gives the lowest interference drag The wing-fuselage junction is aerodynamically cleanest — but the carry-through structure sits right where the payload should be.
  • A high wing augments the effective dihedral, a low wing works against it Therefore low-wing aircraft need more geometric dihedral for the same roll stability.
  • A strut-braced wing has particularly low drag

Which functional principles do high-lift devices use?

  • Increase of wing camber The basic effect of any flap: shifting the cl-α curve upwards.
  • Increase of wing area Fowler motion — the flap translates aft before deflecting.
  • Boundary layer control through improved pressure distribution or momentum supply Slots feed high-energy air onto the upper surface; active systems blow or suck.
  • Removal of the "spoiled" boundary layer Also a boundary-layer control mechanism, mainly used in active systems.
  • Reduction of wing area to increase the wing loading

Passive devices (surfaces placed into the flow) are widely used; active devices (suction or blowing) remain rare.

Why are leading-edge devices such as slats usually combined with powerful trailing-edge flaps?

  • Trailing-edge flaps shift the stall to a lower angle of attack; the leading-edge device compensates this shift A plain or slotted flap raises cl but reduces αstall. A slat delays separation and restores (or increases) the stall angle.
  • The combination delivers a considerably higher usable CL,max than either device alone The full benefit of the trailing-edge device can only be exploited if the wing does not stall before the flap's lift increment is reached.
  • Because slats alone produce the largest camber increase
  • Because leading-edge devices reduce the drag of the flapped wing

Careful with the diagrams: the sketched cl-α curves are 2D airfoil data referred to the clean wing chord line and the clean reference area — they must still be corrected for a swept 3D wing.

Raymer's method estimates the maximum lift of a partially flapped wing. Which statements are correct?

CL,max ≈ 0.9 · [ cl,max,flapped·(Sflapped/Sref) + cl,max,unflapped·(Sunflapped/Sref) ]

  • The 2D section values are weighted with their respective area shares This is the core idea: the wing is split into strips, each with its own maximum lift.
  • The factor 0.9 is a crude correction for 3D effects It accounts for the fact that a 3D wing never reaches the 2D value.
  • For a swept wing an additional factor cos(Λc/4) is applied CL,max ≈ 0.9·cos(Λc/4)·Σ cl,max,i·Si/Sref.
  • The lift increment is only obtained on the flapped part of the wing A frequent mistake in exercises is to apply Δcl to the whole wing.
  • The method is accurate enough to replace wind tunnel tests

Watch the reference area when symmetry is exploited: if only the half wing is discretised, the strips must sum to Sref/2.

Which considerations limit the size of the flapped area?

  • Sufficient span must remain for the ailerons to guarantee roll control The classic conflict between the high-lift group and the flight-mechanics group.
  • Thrust gates interrupt the flap system and reduce its effectiveness High-lift devices work best when they are continuous; engine exhaust cut-outs break them up.
  • The flapped area should be kept as small as possible for aerodynamic reasons
  • Blown flaps solve all layout constraints without penalty

Which typical flap deflection angles are used?

  • 20° – 40° for take-off A compromise: enough lift for the field length, but not so much drag that the climb gradient is lost.
  • 60° – 70° for landing Here the extra drag is welcome, because it steepens the approach and shortens the landing roll.
  • Light aircraft often use no flaps at all for take-off Their field performance is usually not flap-limited and the drag penalty would hurt the climb.
  • 75° is the standard take-off setting because it gives maximum lift

Raymer gives ΔCD0,flap = Fflap·(cflap/c)·(Sflapped/Sref)·(δflap − 10°). Which statements are correct?

  • Fflap = 0.0144 for plain flaps and 0.0074 for slotted flaps Slotted flaps produce roughly half the parasite drag increment of a plain flap at the same setting.
  • The induced drag increment follows ΔCDi,flap = kflap²·(ΔCL,flap)²·cos(Λc/4) With kflap = 0.14 for full-span and 0.28 for half-span flaps: a partial-span flap creates a much more distorted lift distribution and therefore more induced drag.
  • The formula is intended for trailing-edge flaps deflected by more than about 20° Below that the linear fit is not valid.
  • High-lift devices only affect the parasite drag

The missed-approach and second-segment climb cases are exactly where these increments matter, because there the aircraft is heavy, slow, and configured.

Which statement best describes the "classic compromise" of wing design that makes high-lift devices necessary?

  • Wings are oversized for cruise but undersized for take-off and landing The wing is sized by the cruise or field-length constraint; without flaps the low-speed lift would demand a far larger wing, which would then be inefficient in cruise.
  • Wings are undersized for cruise but oversized for take-off and landing
  • A single fixed wing geometry is optimal for both cruise and low speed
  • High-lift devices are used mainly because they reduce wing weight

For first estimates, assume that the flap weight is contained in the wing weight.

Which load share should the nose gear of a tricycle landing gear carry?

  • 40 – 50 %
  • 1 – 3 %

Which are genuine advantages of a tricycle landing gear over a taildragger?

  • Directional stability on the ground A disturbance produces a restoring reaction; on a taildragger the same disturbance tightens the turn — the classic ground loop.
  • Better view from the cockpit and more comfort for passengers The fuselage sits horizontally on the ground.
  • Good behaviour in crosswind landings and stable two-point landings at high angle of attack No tendency to "jump" as with a taildragger two-point landing.
  • Lower landing gear weight
  • Lower drag for a fixed gear

Disadvantage of the tricycle gear: a complex design (steering, dampers) and a large, heavy and expensive nose gear.

Which landing gear configuration fits which application?

  • Single main gear → sailplanes and some high-altitude reconnaissance aircraft Very simple, light and easy to retract into the fuselage — at the price of poor ground stability and control.
  • Bicycle gear → rarely used, mainly military and VTOL aircraft Two main gears on the symmetry axis at roughly equal distance from the c.g., plus outriggers.
  • Quadricycle gear → very heavy cargo or bomber aircraft Allows a low cabin floor for easy loading and needs no cut-outs in the wing.
  • Multi-bogey gear → large transport aircraft Spreads the load over the pavement and adds redundancy.
  • Taildragger gear → modern large transport jets

Single main gear, bicycle and quadricycle share one drawback: rotation is limited, so take-off happens at low α and the take-off run becomes longer.

Why does CS-25 require at least two wheels per main landing gear strut?

  • Redundancy in case of a tyre failure The bogey arrangement enhances safety by providing redundancy — a burst tyre must not end the landing.
  • Distribution of the load over a larger pavement area This is the general reason for multi-bogey gears on large aircraft: the airport pavement classification limits the load per wheel.
  • To reduce the aerodynamic drag of the gear
  • To reduce the landing gear weight

Which statements about shock absorption are correct?

  • The energy of the vertical velocity is absorbed by the shock absorber, the energy of the horizontal velocity by the brakes Two separate energy paths — sizing the gear means sizing both.
  • The oleo shock strut is the most common absorber type An oil-gas strut: the gas acts as spring, the metered orifice as damper.
  • A rigid axle relies solely on the tyre to absorb the shock Used on light sailplanes, where landing energies are small.
  • Solid spring, levered bungee and triangulated gears all tend to cause tyre scrubbing The wheel moves sideways or fore-aft during deflection, which wears the tyres.
  • A trailing link gear is unsuitable for rough field operations

The landing gear weight is to be distributed between nose and main gear. Which shares are typical?

  • General aviation: 75 – 80 % main gear, 20 – 25 % nose gear Small aircraft have a relatively heavier nose gear share.
  • Large transport: 85 – 92 % main gear, 8 – 15 % nose gear Same trend as the load distribution: the bigger the aircraft, the more dominant the main gear.
  • Fighters: 50 % main gear, 50 % nose gear
  • Military transports: 60 % main gear, 40 % nose gear

The split matters because most Class I and Class II methods only deliver the total gear weight, while the c.g. calculation needs the individual positions.

Gear drag is estimated from frontal area times a drag coefficient. Which values are correct?

  • Regular wheel and tyre: CD ≈ 0.25 The baseline value; a second wheel in tandem only adds about 0.15 because it sits in the wake of the first.
  • Wheel and tyre with fairing: CD ≈ 0.13 Roughly half the drag of the bare wheel — which is why fixed-gear aircraft use wheel pants.
  • Flat spring gear leg: CD ≈ 1.4 A bluff body in cross-flow — one of the worst offenders, comparable to a fork or bogey fitting.
  • Streamline strut: CD ≈ 0.05 Compared with about 0.30 for a round strut or wire — a factor of six for the same structural job.
  • Round strut or wire: CD ≈ 0.02

Gear drag matters most for the missed-approach L/D of a retractable-gear aircraft and for the whole cruise of a fixed-gear aircraft.

  • Only after the empennage layout and sizing (Chapter 11) is complete The gear position depends on the c.g. — and the c.g. position (and the wing position relative to the fuselage) is only fixed by the stability and control analysis.
  • Immediately in Chapter 10, since the design rules are purely geometric
  • Right at the end of the whole design process, after the three-view
  • Already during preliminary sizing (Chapter 5)

What is the purpose of the empennage and its control surfaces?

  • To provide trim Equilibrium of moments about the c.g. must be achievable for every permissible combination of c.g. position, flap setting, speed and engine condition.
  • To provide stability The tendency to return to the original flight state after a disturbance.
  • To provide control Enough control power for the required manoeuvrability, without excessive control forces.
  • To generate the main part of the lift

All three tasks are realised by generating aerodynamic moments about the total centre of gravity.

How are the tail volume coefficients defined?

  • CH = (SH/SW) · (lH/cMAC) The horizontal tail is referenced to the mean aerodynamic chord, because it works about the pitch axis.
  • CV = (SV/SW) · (lV/b) The vertical tail is referenced to the wing span, because it works about the yaw axis.
  • CH = (SH/SW) · (lH/b)
  • CV = SV · lV, with the dimension of a volume

lH and lV are the distances from the neutral point of the respective stabilizer to the total aircraft c.g. The tail volume is a measure of the effectiveness of a stabilizer with respect to both its size and its position.

Two designs have the same horizontal tail volume coefficient, but design B has a 20 % longer tail lever arm. What follows?

  • Design B needs a roughly 17 % smaller horizontal tail area SH ~ 1/lH for constant CH: a factor 1/1.2 ≈ 0.83.
  • Design B saves structural weight and drag in the tail Exactly the reason for the design rule "extend the lever arms as far as the available fuselage allows".
  • Design B has a much less effective tail
  • The longer lever arm always makes the aircraft heavier overall

The limit is the available fuselage length and the resulting structural and c.g. consequences.

Which design rules apply to stabilizer surfaces?

  • Use symmetric airfoils to keep trim changes with speed small A cambered stabilizer would produce a speed-dependent moment that constantly needs re-trimming.
  • Avoid placing the stabilizer in the propeller or jet stream Reasons: induced vibrations and structural fatigue, cabin noise, trim changes with engine setting, and hot jet plumes damaging the structure.
  • The stabilizer must stall well after the main wing Controllability must be ensured at all times — achieved by low aspect ratio, a gently stalling airfoil, and a higher critical Mach number.
  • For transonic aircraft: sweep about 5° higher and t/c about 10 % lower than the outboard wing This raises the critical Mach number of the tail by roughly ΔMacrit = 0.05 relative to the wing.
  • The rule about the propeller stream has no exceptions

Which sequence describes the typical empennage design procedure?

The answers refer to the order of the steps, so they are not shuffled.

  1. Configuration decision → sizing via tail volume coefficients → initial wing position guess → final sizing from trim and stability → iterate if the deviation exceeds 10 % The statistical estimate provides the starting point; the stability and control analysis provides the real answer; the loop is closed if they disagree by more than about 10 %.
  2. Trim and stability analysis first, then the wing position, then the tail volume estimate
  3. Tail volume coefficients only; a further analysis is unnecessary
  4. Accept the result of the trim and stability analysis regardless of how far it deviates from the statistical estimate

Which flight states are critical for the sizing of the horizontal stabilizer?

  • The critical combination of c.g. position, flap setting and speed Typically the most forward c.g. with maximum flap deflection in the landing configuration.
  • Take-off rotation The tail must be able to rotate the aircraft about the main gear at VR.
  • One engine inoperative on a multi-engine aircraft
  • Spin recovery

Depending on the engine position, the sizing case differs: for engines below the c.g. the flare in landing configuration with the most forward c.g. is critical; for engines above the c.g. it is the go-around.

Which flight states are critical for the sizing of the vertical stabilizer?

  • Engine failure on a multi-engine aircraft Usually the design case: the yawing moment from the asymmetric thrust plus the drag of the dead engine must be balanced down to VMC.
  • Take-off and landing in crosswind The rudder must be able to align the aircraft with the runway.
  • Spin recovery Requires unblanketed rudder area — at least about one third of the rudder should stay in clean flow.
  • Symmetric cruise flight at maximum speed

The vertical stabilizer is sized for one engine inoperative. Which contributions make up the yawing moment to be balanced?

  • The thrust moment of the operating engine, NE = Ttake-off · yE Asymmetric thrust times the lateral engine offset.
  • The drag moment of the failed engine, ND Depends strongly on the engine type: windmilling fixed-pitch propeller ≈ 0.8·blade area·yE·q, feathered variable-pitch propeller ≈ 0.1·blade area·yE·q, jet engine ≈ 0.3·frontal area·yE·q.
  • The rolling moment due to the bank angle
  • The case is uncritical because it occurs at high speed

SV = (NE + ND) / (½·ρ·vMC²·lV·cY,V), where cY,V is the maximum side force coefficient including rudder deflection.

A feathered propeller produces roughly an eighth of the drag moment of a windmilling one — which is why feathering is such a decisive safety feature.

What are dorsal and ventral fins used for?

  • A dorsal fin increases directional stability at high sideslip angles The vortex forming on the fin delays the fin stall, and the added area helps spin prevention and recovery.
  • A ventral fin increases directional stability at high angles of attack When the vertical tail is blanketed by the fuselage, the ventral fin still sees clean airflow — very useful for spin prevention and recovery.
  • Both are used to deliberately reduce directional stability
  • Both mainly increase roll stability

Which statements about the position of the horizontal tail relative to the wing wake are correct?

  • At high angles of attack, but before the stall, the wake hitting the tail is desirable as a natural stall warning The resulting buffet and stick shake tells the pilot that the stall is approaching.
  • In the fully stalled condition the tail must not be immersed in the wing wake Otherwise the stabilizer loses effectiveness exactly when it is needed, which can lead to an unrecoverable pitch-up — the deep stall.
  • The position of the horizontal tail relative to the vertical tail matters for spin recovery The horizontal tail must not blanket the rudder; at least about one third of the rudder area should remain in clean flow.
  • The wing wake has no relevant effect on stabilizer efficiency

Which guidelines apply to elevator and rudder sizing in conceptual design?

  • They usually extend from close to the root over about 90 – 100 % of the stabilizer half span Unlike ailerons, control surfaces on the empennage normally use nearly the whole span.
  • The chord ratio is chosen from statistical guidelines at this stage The exact chord follows only from a detailed flight-mechanical analysis in later phases.
  • Statistical values are sufficient; no further analysis is required
  • Large aircraft trim exclusively with trim tabs

How is the wing position relative to the fuselage determined?

  • By splitting the aircraft into a wing group and a fuselage group and solving for xLEMAC xLEMAC = xFG − xCG,LEMAC + (mWG/mFG)·(xWG,LEMAC − xCG,LEMAC).
  • The wing group typically contains wing, main gear, engines and nacelles The fuselage group contains fuselage, nose gear, empennage and systems. Which components belong where depends on where they are actually attached.
  • A good first assumption is xCG,LEMAC = 0.3 · MAC The distance of the total c.g. behind the leading edge of the mean aerodynamic chord — the starting value for the whole iteration.
  • The total c.g. itself follows from xCG = Σ(mi·xi) / Σmi Which is why the component masses of Chapters 6 to 10 all have to be available before the empennage can be sized properly.
  • The wing is placed at the geometric centre of the fuselage

Which flight state gives the critical trim case for the horizontal stabilizer?

  • Engine below the total c.g.: the flare in approach (landing) configuration, maximum flap setting, most forward c.g. A low-mounted engine produces a nose-up moment; at idle thrust in the flare this support disappears exactly when the flapped wing demands the largest tail download.
  • Engine above the total c.g.: the go-around in approach configuration, maximum flap setting, most forward c.g. A high-mounted engine produces a nose-down moment, which becomes largest when go-around thrust is applied.
  • Take-off rotation is the second critical state for the horizontal stabilizer The tail must rotate the aircraft about the main gear at VR — a case that couples empennage sizing to the landing gear position.
  • The pitching moment of the fuselage MF must never be neglected
  • The stabilizer is sized for the dynamic stability requirement

Which typical static margins are used, and how does the static margin enter the sizing?

  • CM,α,CG = −CL,α · (static margin) Instead of merely demanding CM,α,CG < 0, a minimum value for the derivative is prescribed — that is what the static margin buys.
  • Transport aircraft: about 5 – 10 % General aviation aircraft sit slightly higher, at about 5 – 15 %.
  • Propeller training aircraft: about 15 – 25 % Training aircraft are deliberately made very stable and forgiving — at the cost of trim drag and control forces.
  • Fighters with relaxed stability: about 0 to −15 % A negative static margin means the aircraft is statically unstable and needs an artificial stability system — the price for manoeuvrability and low trim drag.
  • Conventional fighters use the largest static margins of all categories

Which design rules apply specifically to the vertical stabilizer?

  • It should use a symmetrical airfoil A cambered fin would produce a permanent side force and a permanent yawing moment in symmetric flight.
  • Subsonic: a sweep angle of less than 20° This achieves a high side force with a small area — sweep reduces the lift curve slope, which is exactly what the fin needs most.
  • Transonic: a sweep angle about 5° higher than the main wing To raise the critical Mach number and avoid shock-induced separation and the resulting loss of control power.
  • At least about one third of the rudder area should remain un-blanketed for spin recovery Which is what drives the position of the horizontal tail relative to the vertical tail.
  • Aesthetic considerations play no role in the design of the vertical tail

What is plotted in a "scissor" diagram?

  • The stabilizer area ratio SH/SW over the c.g. position (relative to the wing aerodynamic centre) Both the trim condition and the static stability condition can be expressed as lines in this plane — their shape gives the diagram its name.
  • Thrust-to-weight ratio over wing loading
  • Payload over range
  • Lift coefficient over drag coefficient

It answers two questions simultaneously: how large must the horizontal tail be, and which c.g. range is permissible for a given tail size.

Which line in the scissor diagram comes from which requirement?

  • The static stability condition gives a straight line through the origin, SH/SW ≈ a · xCG-AC It follows from setting CM,α,CG = 0 and defines the aft c.g. limit: the further aft the c.g., the more tail area is needed.
  • The trim condition gives a line with an additional constant term from CM,W and CM,E SH/SW = CL,W/(CL,H·ηH·lH/cMAC)·xCG-AC + (CM,W+CM,E)/(CL,H·ηH·lH/cMAC). It defines the forward c.g. limit.
  • The two lines together enclose the permissible combinations of tail area and c.g. position For a chosen SH/SW the horizontal cut between the two lines gives the allowable c.g. range.
  • Only the stability line has to be respected; the trim line is optional

The intersection of the two lines marks the smallest theoretically possible horizontal tail area — at which, however, the permissible c.g. range shrinks to a single point.

The slope of the stability line is a = CL,α,W / [ CL,α,H · ηH · (1 − dε/dα) · lH/cMAC ]. Which parameters therefore influence the scissor diagram?

  • The tail lever arm lH and the mean aerodynamic chord cMAC A longer lever arm flattens the stability line, so less tail area is needed for the same c.g. position.
  • The downwash gradient dε/dα A strong downwash gradient reduces the effective angle-of-attack change at the tail and therefore its stabilizing contribution — the required area grows.
  • The dynamic pressure ratio ηH A tail in a strongly disturbed flow is less effective and needs more area.
  • The ratio of the lift curve slopes of wing and tail A wing with a high CL,α is more destabilising for an aft c.g., a tail with a high CL,α is more effective.
  • The wing loading W/S

Additional parameters entering the trim line: the zero-lift pitching moment of the wing CM,W (and hence the airfoil camber and flap setting), the engine moment CM,E, and the maximum usable tail lift coefficient CL,H.

A required static margin is introduced instead of using the neutral point directly. What happens in the scissor diagram?

  • The stability line shifts, so that a given tail area allows a c.g. only further forward CM,α,CG = −CL,α·(static margin): demanding a genuinely negative CM,α instead of just zero moves the aft c.g. limit forward.
  • For an unchanged c.g. range, a larger horizontal tail is required Stability margin has to be paid for with tail area, weight and drag.
  • The condition CM,α,CG = 0 is the design goal for a stable aircraft
  • The static margin only affects the forward c.g. limit

Modern fighters deliberately go the other way: relaxed static stability with a c.g. behind the neutral point, made flyable by a computerised flight control system, and rewarded with a much smaller tail and less trim drag.

During the design the flap deflection for landing is increased, which makes the wing pitching moment CM,W considerably more negative. What is the effect?

  • The trim line shifts, and a larger horizontal tail is required for the same forward c.g. The nose-down moment of the flapped wing has to be balanced by a larger tail download — the trim case, not the stability case, becomes more demanding.
  • The permissible forward c.g. limit moves aft for an unchanged tail area The trim line is the forward boundary of the usable c.g. range.
  • The slope of the stability line changes
  • Flap setting is irrelevant for empennage sizing

This coupling is one reason why high-lift design (Chapter 9) and empennage sizing (Chapter 11) have to be iterated together.

Which condition describes longitudinal static stability?

  • CM,α,CG = ∂CM,CG/∂α < 0 An increase in angle of attack must produce a nose-down moment, which drives the aircraft back to its original state.
  • ∂CM,CG/∂α > 0
  • ∂CM,CG/∂α = 0
  • CM,CG = 0 at every angle of attack

Static stability means all moments generated in the disturbed position tend to restore the original flight state. Dynamic stability additionally requires that the resulting oscillation is damped.

Static stability is only a necessary, not a sufficient condition for dynamic stability.

How do the individual contributions to longitudinal stability act?

  • The wing is destabilising when the c.g. lies behind the aerodynamic centre The term CL,α,W·xCG-AC is positive for an aft c.g., pushing CM,α towards instability.
  • The horizontal stabilizer is stabilising Its lift increment acts at a long lever arm behind the c.g. and produces a nose-down moment when α increases.
  • The downwash of the wing reduces the tail contribution by the factor (1 − dε/dα) The tail only feels part of the free-stream angle-of-attack change.
  • The fuselage is strongly stabilising in pitch

Full expression: CM,α,CG = CL,α,W·xCG-AC − CL,α,H·ηH·(SH/SW)·(1 − dε/dα)·(lH/cMAC − xCG-AC)

Which statements about lateral-directional static stability are correct?

  • Directional static stability requires that a positive sideslip angle β produces a positive yawing moment about the c.g. CN,β > 0 — the aircraft turns into the relative wind, like a weathervane.
  • The fuselage contribution is usually destabilising CN,β,F < 0, which is precisely what the vertical stabilizer has to overcome.
  • The wing contribution can be neglected for small sideslip angles A simplification used in the conceptual sizing of the fin.
  • The vertical stabilizer contributes destabilising yawing moments

Solving the moment balance for the fin area gives SV/SW = [(CN,β − CN,β,F)/(−CY,β,V)] · (bW/lV).

An aircraft has a strong effective dihedral (high wing plus aft sweep plus geometric dihedral). Which consequences follow?

  • The spiral mode is stabilised The strong roll-due-to-sideslip effect levels the aircraft again.
  • The Dutch roll mode is destabilised The same effect couples roll and yaw and reduces the damping of the Dutch roll oscillation.
  • Anhedral may be required as a design fix Which is exactly why many high-wing, swept transports (military airlifters) show noticeable anhedral.
  • The longitudinal static stability is improved

This is a good example of why "more stability" is not automatically better: the two lateral modes have opposite preferences.

What does the "loading envelope" describe and why does it matter?

  • The permissible combinations of take-off weight and c.g. position Depending on payload, fuel load and loading arrangement, the aircraft must operate safely over a whole range of weights and c.g. positions.
  • It defines the c.g. range that the empennage sizing must cover The tail must trim the most forward and remain stable at the most aft c.g. of the whole envelope, not just at one nominal point.
  • It later becomes the basis for the load and trim sheet of the operator The operational document that ensures each individual flight stays inside the certified envelope.
  • It is enough to size the empennage for a single design c.g. position

The drag polar is written as CD = CD0 + (CL − CL0)²/(π·A·e). Which statements are correct?

  • CD0 is the lift-independent (zero-lift) drag Skin friction plus form drag plus miscellaneous contributions.
  • The second term is the lift-dependent (induced) drag It grows with the square of the lift coefficient — which is why induced drag dominates at low speed and parasite drag at high speed.
  • A cambered airfoil shifts the minimum of the polar to CL0 ≠ 0 The polar is no longer symmetric about CL = 0; the aircraft has minimum drag at a positive lift coefficient.
  • Increasing the aspect ratio A reduces the induced drag at a given CL A appears in the denominator.
  • e is always exactly 1

Which condition holds at the point of maximum aerodynamic efficiency (L/D)max?

  • The induced drag equals the zero-lift drag Setting d(CD/CL)/dCL = 0 gives CDi = CD0 — half of the total drag is induced, half is parasite.
  • The corresponding lift coefficient is CL = √(π·A·e·CD0) Directly from equating the two drag contributions.
  • The maximum efficiency is (L/D)max = ½·√(π·A·e/CD0) This single formula shows the two levers: increase the aspect ratio, or reduce the zero-lift drag.
  • It occurs at the maximum lift coefficient
  • It occurs at zero lift, where the drag is smallest

Geometrically, (L/D)max is the point where a straight line from the origin is tangent to the drag polar.

How does the component build-up method determine the zero-lift drag?

CD0 = Σ (Cf,i · FFi · Qi · Swet,i) / Sref + CD,misc

  • Each component is represented by a flat plate with the same wetted area and Reynolds number This gives the flat-plate skin friction coefficient Cf.
  • The form factor FF accounts for the drag due to the actual shape Pressure drag caused by thickness — it depends on t/c or fineness ratio and on Mach number.
  • The factor Q accounts for interference drag The mutual influence of touching bodies; it is highly complex and is therefore approximated statistically.
  • Miscellaneous drag must be added separately for flaps, gear, engine installation and cooling Engine installation and cooling drag, for instance, may be estimated at around 10 % of the total CD0.
  • Small components can be neglected without consequence

Which aerodynamic analysis methods are suitable for drag estimation in conceptual design?

  • Handbook (build-up) methods such as DATCOM Quick, reasonably accurate, easily implemented in a spreadsheet — and still the method of choice for drag polars in early design.
  • RANS methods Suited for drag, but with computation times of hours per case they are used for spot-checks rather than for design sweeps.
  • DES / LES methods Physically suitable, but days to weeks of computing time make them impractical in conceptual design.
  • Vortex lattice / full potential methods
  • Euler methods

The general trade-off is between accuracy and computation time. Because shear forces on the wetted surfaces and locally confined flow features dominate the drag build-up, handbook methods remain competitive.

Which measures actually improve the aerodynamic performance (L/D) of an aircraft?

  • Increasing the aspect ratio Reduces induced drag; the price is structural weight, so a trade study is needed.
  • Reducing the wetted area relative to the reference area CD0 scales directly with Swet/Sref — this is the fundamental argument behind blended and flying-wing configurations.
  • Winglets or well-designed wing tips They primarily reduce vortex drag; as a side effect they add roll stability.
  • Promoting laminar flow, e.g. with 6-series or natural laminar flow airfoils A laminar boundary layer produces far less skin friction — but the benefit is fragile (surface quality, contamination, Reynolds number).
  • Reducing interference drag at wing-fuselage and tail-fuselage junctions Fairings and fillets attack the Q factor of the build-up method.
  • Choosing the wing position with the highest interference drag
Be sceptical about unproven drag-saving technology — new winglets, laminar flow control and similar promises have disappointed even experienced companies.

Which statements about the critical Mach number and the drag divergence Mach number are correct?

  • At Ma* the local flow first reaches Ma = 1 somewhere on the airfoil This is the definition of the critical Mach number; from there on regions of supersonic flow develop.
  • MaDD > Ma* The strong drag rise does not start at Ma* but somewhat above it, once the shocks have become strong enough.
  • Ma* depends on airfoil shape, thickness ratio and sweep angle The three main design levers for transonic wing design.
  • Supercritical airfoils are used to increase Ma* They spread the suction over the upper surface so that a higher free-stream Mach number can be flown before strong shocks form.
  • Above MaDD the drag decreases again

A further transonic problem is buffet: shock oscillations that excite the structure (buffeting) and cause fatigue.

What does the area rule state?

  • At transonic and supersonic speeds the wave drag of a wing-body combination is similar to that of a body of revolution with the same cross-sectional area distribution Hence the "coke-bottle" fuselage waisting where the wing joins.
  • The minimum wave drag is achieved for a shape as close as possible to the Sears-Haack body The smooth area distribution of the Sears-Haack body is the theoretical optimum.
  • The area rule minimises the skin friction drag
  • Wave drag can be predicted very accurately with simple handbook formulas

Which drag contributions belong to which category?

  • Skin friction and form drag: lift-independent Together they make up the bulk of CD0 in subsonic flight.
  • Induced (vortex) drag: lift-dependent, caused by 3D effects CDi = CL²/(πAe). It does not exist in 2D flow — which is why airfoils can reach L/D values above 100.
  • Trim drag: caused by the lift the tailplane must produce to trim the aircraft Partly induced drag, partly profile drag from the control surface deflection; it can only be determined together with the flight-mechanical analysis.
  • Interference drag: additional drag from the flow around touching bodies Treated statistically through the factor Q.
  • Wave drag: always present, even in low-speed flight

Why can a 2D airfoil reach L/D values above 100, while a complete aircraft rarely exceeds about 20?

  • There is no induced drag in 2D flow Induced drag is a consequence of the finite span and the resulting tip vortices — the dominant penalty of a real wing at high lift.
  • The complete aircraft adds fuselage, empennage, nacelles, gear and interference drag Every additional component adds wetted area and therefore parasite drag, without adding lift.
  • The parabolic shape of the 2D drag polar comes only from separation at higher angles of attack Not from induced drag, as is often wrongly assumed.
  • Because a 2D airfoil has no skin friction drag

Consequence for design: the required 2D airfoil L/D depends heavily on the 3D wing geometry — determining it is not a trivial task.

Two designs for the same mission are compared. Design A has CD0 = 0.020 and A·e = 12; design B has CD0 = 0.025 and A·e = 16. Which statements are correct?

(L/D)max = ½·√(π·A·e/CD0)

  • Design B has the higher maximum aerodynamic efficiency A: ½·√(π·12/0.020) = ½·√1885 ≈ 21.7. B: ½·√(π·16/0.025) = ½·√2011 ≈ 22.4.
  • A low CD0 alone is not a measure of aerodynamic quality Only the combination of CD0 and A·e determines (L/D)max. Here the higher aspect ratio more than compensates the higher zero-lift drag.
  • Both designs have exactly the same (L/D)max
  • Design A wins, because a lower CD0 is always better
  • The comparison of (L/D)max is sufficient to pick the better design

Both effects also carry weight penalties in opposite directions, so the final choice still needs a full sizing loop.

Which conclusions should be drawn from the "lessons learned" on weight and drag prediction?

  • Under-predicting drag has the same consequences as under-predicting weight Both destroy the performance guarantees on which the whole project was sold.
  • Claims about new, unproven technology deserve serious scepticism New winglets, laminar flow control, VTOL technology, composite structures, box-wing configurations — all classic sources of optimistic predictions.
  • Estimates should be cross-checked with a second, independent method Especially when the design deviates significantly from the reference aircraft the statistics were derived from.
  • Experienced companies are immune to optimistic predictions

Which sub-questions does preliminary sizing answer?

The key question is: "How big do I need to draw the aircraft?"

  • How much wing area do I need? Follows from the wing loading W/S together with the take-off mass.
  • How big are the engines? Follows from T/W (jets) or P/W (props) together with the take-off mass.
  • How much fuel volume do I need to provide? Follows from the fuel mass fraction determined by the mission analysis.
  • Which spar and rib layout does the wing need?
  • How large must the horizontal stabilizer be?

The outputs of preliminary sizing are: T/W = TTO/(mMTO·g), W/S = mMTO/S, plus mMTO, mF and mOE.

Which statements about the possible approaches to preliminary sizing are correct?

  • A purely statistical approach lacks accuracy, but is always possible Even with almost no data you can always fall back on statistics — which is why it is the safety net of conceptual design.
  • A purely theoretical approach is not feasible Too many required inputs simply do not exist yet at this stage.
  • In practice a mixture of statistical and theoretical methods is applied Which mixture is chosen depends strongly on the configuration and on the data available.
  • Neither the sequence nor the methods of preliminary sizing are unique There is no single correct approach — the sequence depends on which methods you picked. If, for instance, the empty-mass method depends on T/W, the matching has to be done first.
  • One standard procedure works for every aircraft type

In which order do configuration idea, sketch and sizing come?

The answers refer to an order, so they are not shuffled.

  1. Configuration idea with specific features → sketch to document the thoughts → sizing → further design process (Chapters 6–11) You cannot size an aircraft you have not conceived: payload, propulsion, aspect ratio, sweep, tail type and high-lift system all change the sizing result.
  2. Sizing first, then choose a configuration that fits the numbers
  3. The sketch is optional and only serves presentation purposes
  4. The wing and empennage design (Chapters 8 and 11) come before preliminary sizing

The 1978 Rockwell study for a B-1 successor is the classic illustration: very different configurations can serve the very same mission — and each one sizes differently.

What is the relation between CL,max and CL,max,Take-off?

  • CL,max,Take-off is about 0.8 × CL,max Because take-off uses a smaller flap setting than landing — full flaps would create too much drag for the required climb gradient.
  • CL,max denotes the absolute maximum lift and is only used for landing It corresponds to the maximum flap deflection.
  • CL,max,Take-off is about 1.2 × CL,max
  • Both are identical, since the same high-lift system is installed

So if a first estimate gives CL,max = 2.6 for landing, use roughly 2.1 for take-off.

According to the [Dubs] table used in the lecture, which 2D maximum lift coefficients can be reached?

Base airfoil: cl,max = 1.45

  • Plain flap: ≈ 2.25 (Δ ≈ 0.80) The simplest and least effective trailing-edge device.
  • Fowler flap: ≈ 2.80 (Δ ≈ 1.35) Same value as the double-slotted flap — Fowler motion adds area on top of camber.
  • Slat alone: ≈ 2.00 (Δ ≈ 0.55) A leading-edge device alone gives a modest lift increment; its main benefit is the delayed stall.

Which statements about the statistical CL,max bands (after [Roskam]) are correct?

  • Higher values are usually found for slower flying aircraft A slow aircraft needs a large CL,max to keep its stall speed down.
  • Lower values are usually found for faster flying aircraft Compare the Lockheed Starfighter (fast, low CL,max) with the Hawker Harrier (slow, high CL,max).
  • For transport jets the clean CL,max lies roughly between 1.2 and 1.8 With about 1.6–2.2 for take-off and about 1.8–2.8 for landing.
  • The clean CL,max differs enormously between aircraft categories

Within preliminary sizing it is entirely legitimate to keep CL,max as a free parameter and compute T/W and W/S for several values — it can then be traded.

Which simplification is applied to the drag polar within preliminary sizing?

  • The zero-angle-of-attack lift coefficient CL0 is neglected CD = CD0 + CL²/(π·AR·e) instead of the full form with (CL − CL0)².
  • The induced drag is neglected
  • The zero-lift drag is neglected
  • The Oswald factor is set to 1

Drag must always be assessed together with lift: for any CL a corresponding CD follows, and their ratio L/D is the measure of aerodynamic efficiency that enters both the T/W constraints and the mass calculation.

Which statements about the airplane efficiency factor e (Oswald factor) are correct?

  • Sailplanes: e ≈ 0.85 … 0.98 The highest values of all categories — clean configuration, high aspect ratio, little interference.
  • Civil jets: e ≈ 0.75 … 0.90 General aviation aircraft sit somewhat lower, at about 0.70 … 0.80.
  • It is a total correction factor that scales the ideal drag-due-to-lift to fit the real aircraft It even absorbs an estimate for trim drag.
  • There is no direct relation to the lift distribution A common misconception. The lift distribution influences e slightly, but the major influence is the overall configuration of the aircraft.
  • Fighter aircraft reach the highest e values of all categories
Named after W. B. Oswald, who first applied the factor in 1932.

  • ≈ 34.7

How does Loftin's method estimate CD0?

  • CD0 is scaled from a reference aircraft by the ratio of wetted-area ratios Assumption: for very similar aircraft concepts, zero-lift drag scales with wetted area.
  • A Reynolds number correction with the exponent 1/6 is applied Accounts for the fact that the skin-friction coefficient falls slowly with increasing Reynolds number.
  • It only works if the reference CD0 is known and the concepts are similar Typical use: known data of a 200-seat aircraft is used to estimate CD0 of a new 150-seat aircraft.
  • No reference aircraft is needed

Required inputs: Swet/Sref for the new aircraft and for the reference, plus the Reynolds numbers of both.

The Boeing B-47 (AR = 9.4) and the Avro Vulcan (AR = 3.0) both reach L/Dmax ≈ 17. How can that be?

  • Both have almost the same wetted aspect ratio b²/Swet B-47 ≈ 1.2, Vulcan ≈ 1.1. That is the parameter that really matters, not the geometric aspect ratio.
  • The Vulcan compensates its low aspect ratio with a much smaller Swet/Sref A delta wing has an enormous reference area relative to its wetted area, so its parasite drag coefficient is very low.
  • Subsonic L/Dmax is mainly governed by wing span and wetted area Induced drag falls with span, parasite drag rises with wetted area — combining both gives b²/Swet.
  • Because the geometric aspect ratio alone determines L/Dmax

wetted aspect ratio = b²/Swet = AR / (Swet/Sref)

Which lift coefficients are used to calculate L/DTO and L/DLanding?

  • CL,TO = CL,max,TO / 1.44 Because vTO = 1.2 · vstall, and lift scales with the square of the speed: 1.2² = 1.44.
  • CL,L = CL,max,L / 1.69 Because vL = 1.3 · vstall: 1.3² = 1.69.
  • CL,max,TO and CL,max,L are used directly
  • Both are divided by the same factor 1.5

L/D = CL / (CD0 + ΔCD0 + CL²/(π·e·AR)), with CD0 and AR from the clean L/Dmax estimate.

Which drag and efficiency increments are used for the take-off and landing configuration?

  • Take-off flaps: ΔCD0 = 0.010 … 0.020, e = 0.70 … 0.80 A modest penalty — take-off settings are deliberately low-drag.
  • Landing flaps: ΔCD0 = 0.055 … 0.075, e = 0.65 … 0.75 Roughly five times the take-off increment. Here the drag is welcome for the approach — but it makes the missed approach the critical climb case.
  • Landing gear: ΔCD0 = 0.015 … 0.025, no effect on e The gear adds parasite drag but does not distort the lift distribution.
  • Clean: ΔCD0 = 0, e = 0.75 … 0.90 The reference configuration.
  • Take-off and landing flaps produce roughly the same ΔCD0

Note that the flap settings reduce e as well — a partial-span flap distorts the spanwise lift distribution.

Which rules govern the choice of the design point in the matching process?

The lecture gives an explicit priority order.

  1. Priority 1: choose the lowest possible T/W (or P/W) A lower T/W means smaller engines and thus lower weight and cost.
  2. Priority 2: choose the highest possible W/S A higher W/S means a smaller wing and thus lower structural weight, less wetted area, less drag.
  3. All values must first be converted to maximum take-off weight and sea level Otherwise the constraint curves are not comparable in one diagram.
  4. The design point may lie in the shaded area if only one requirement is violated

Civil aircraft under CS-23 and CS-25 are mainly sized by landing and take-off field length plus climb and cruise requirements. Military aircraft may additionally be driven by manoeuvrability (fighters) or loiter (surveillance).

Which statements about the energy approach to aircraft performance are correct?

  • The energy height he = h + v²/(2g) equals the altitude the aircraft would reach if its speed were traded for height without energy loss The specific energy has the unit of a distance, hence the name "energy height".
  • Ps = dhe/dt = dh/dt + (v/g)·dv/dt is the specific excess power It is the time rate at which the aircraft gains altitude or speed.
  • Ps = 0 means steady horizontal flight, or an exchange of altitude for airspeed Ps < 0 means the aircraft decelerates and/or descends, Ps > 0 means it accelerates and/or climbs.
  • Lift adds no power to the aircraft system By definition lift acts normal to the velocity vector, so it does no work. Only thrust adds and drag subtracts power.
  • The load factor does not appear in the energy equation

The energy-manoeuvrability equation reads T/W = q·CD0/(W/S) + n²/(π·AR·e) · (1/q) · (W/S) + Ps/v. Which statements are correct?

  • The first term is the parasite drag contribution and falls with increasing W/S A small wing means little wetted area for a given weight.
  • The second term is the induced drag contribution and rises with increasing W/S A small wing has to work at a high lift coefficient. The two competing terms are what makes the cruise constraint a U-shaped curve with a minimum.
  • Cruise, climb and turning flight are all special cases of this single equation Cruise: n = 1, Ps = 0. Steady climb: n = 1, Ps = climb rate. Turn: n = 1/cos(bank angle).
  • Every such requirement yields a minimum value for T/W Unlike the landing and stall constraints, which yield a maximum W/S.
  • The equation cannot be simplified further

Example for a shallow cruise turn at 15° bank: n = 1/cos(15°) = 1.035.

Which climb gradients does CS-25.121 require with one engine inoperative?

Which statements about the take-off field length constraint are correct?

  • The requirement is converted into a ground roll distance sG, because energy-based methods work more easily with it For CS-23 aircraft the total take-off distance is roughly 30–40 % higher than sG, for CS-25 aircraft roughly 25–30 % higher.
  • The mean acceleration during the ground roll depends on available T/W, aerodynamic drag and rolling friction These are the three terms of the resulting T/W equation.
  • The velocity at the middle of the ground run is vGR = vTO/√2 Follows from constant acceleration: after half the distance the aircraft has reached 1/√2 of the lift-off speed. This is the speed used for the mean dynamic pressure.
  • The equation yields a minimum value for T/W Like all thrust-driven constraints.
  • The lift-off speed is independent of the wing loading

Which input data are used for the take-off ground run calculation?

  • CL,GR ≈ 0.15 The lift coefficient during the ground roll of a tricycle-gear aircraft, sitting nearly level.
  • CD,GR = CD0 + ΔCD0,TO Induced drag is neglected because CL,GR is small (below 0.2).
  • Rolling friction on dry concrete or asphalt: μ ≈ 0.02 … 0.05 With brakes on the same surface gives 0.30–0.50 — that is the landing case. Soft turf and wet grass reach about 0.07–0.08 rolling.
  • For a taildragger with CL,GR above 0.2 the induced drag must be included CD,GR = CD0 + ΔCD0,TO + CDi,TO. A taildragger sits at a high angle of attack during the whole ground run.
  • The ground friction coefficient is the same for every runway surface

Raymer gives the landing field length as sLFL = 5·(W/S)·1/[(ρ/ρ0)·CL,max,L] + sa. Which statements are correct?

  • sa is the obstacle clearance distance: 300 m for airliners on a 3° glideslope 180 m for a GA-type power-off approach and 135 m for STOL on a 7° glideslope.
  • Rearranged, the equation yields a maximum permissible wing loading W/S = (sLFL − sa)/5 · (ρ/ρ0) · CL,max,L — hence the vertical line in the matching diagram.
  • Civil certification specifications do not allow credit for thrust reversers Although reversers or reversible-pitch props do improve the real deceleration, CS-25 instead requires a safety margin of two-thirds (factor 1.67).
  • A higher CL,max,L permits a higher wing loading Which is exactly the feedback loop to the high-lift system design in Chapter 9.
  • The landing field length grows with the square of the wing loading

Why must the constraint values be corrected to reference conditions, and how?

  • Because each constraint was calculated at its own flight condition, and only take-off at ISA MSL makes them comparable Otherwise you would be plotting apples against oranges in one diagram.
  • (T/W)take-off = (T/W)cruise · (Wcruise/Wtake-off) · (Ttake-off/Tcruise) Two effects: the aircraft is lighter in cruise because fuel has been burnt, and the engine delivers less thrust at altitude and speed.
  • (W/S)take-off = (W/S)landing · (Wtake-off/Wlanding) The landing constraint is evaluated at landing weight, which is lower than MTOW.
  • The landing field length and missed-approach climb constraints use the landing weight Cruise, take-off field length and after-take-off climb use WTO; manoeuvring and stall speed use whatever the requirement specifies.
  • The weight ratio can always be set to 1 without significant error

How is T/W converted to P/W for a propeller aircraft, and which efficiencies apply?

  • T = ηprop · P · 1000 / v, with P in kW and v in m/s The ideal thrust P/v is reduced by the propeller efficiency.
  • The propeller efficiency depends on speed, so different values apply to cruise and to take-off Example single-engine aircraft: about 0.80 at high speed (cruise, manoeuvre) and about 0.70 at low speed (take-off, loiter).
  • For turboprops the available power is assumed to fall with the density ratio: P = P0·σ A simple and acceptable approximation.
  • For piston engines the Gagg and Ferrar model is used: P = P0·(σ − 0.117)/0.883 Piston engines lose power slightly faster than the density ratio alone would suggest.
  • Propeller engine manufacturers sell thrust, so no conversion is needed

For climb phases the efficiency can be interpolated between the low-speed and high-speed values.

Which typical installed thrust- or power-to-weight ratios are correct?

  • Jet fighter (dogfighter): T/W ≈ 0.9 The highest value of all categories — manoeuvrability requires excess thrust.
  • Jet transport: T/W ≈ 0.25 … 0.4 The higher value applies to aircraft with fewer engines, because the OEI case is more demanding.
  • Military cargo/bomber: T/W ≈ 0.25 Efficiency matters more than agility.
  • Twin turboprop: P/W ≈ 0.33 kW/kg Compared with about 0.12 kW/kg for a general aviation single and 0.07 kW/kg for a powered sailplane.
  • Powered sailplane: P/W ≈ 0.30 kW/kg

Which typical wing loadings (after [Howe]) are correct?

  • Ultralight aircraft: 200 – 400 N/m² Extremely low — that is what makes their stall speeds so low.
  • Light single piston engine aircraft: 500 – 800 N/m² Roughly 50–80 kg/m².
  • Long haul jet transport: 6200 – 7000+ N/m² About an order of magnitude above an ultralight — the reason such aircraft need elaborate high-lift systems.
  • Land-based strike/interceptor aircraft: 4000 – 5000 N/m² Naval versions sit lower, at about 3500–4000 N/m², because of the carrier approach speed.
  • Small executive jets: ≈ 1000 N/m²
Rough conversion: divide N/m² by 9.81 to obtain kg/m².

The maximum take-off mass is calculated as mMTO = mPL / (1 − mF/mMTO − mOE/mMTO). Which statements are correct?

  • The payload mass comes from the requirements It is the only absolute mass in the equation — everything else is a fraction.
  • The fuel fraction comes from the mission analysis and statistics Segment fuel fractions from tables, plus Breguet equations for cruise and loiter.
  • Assuming mOE/mMTO = const. (Torenbeek) avoids iteration but is less accurate The fraction is then independent of the result.
  • Assuming mOE = f(mMTO) (Raymer, Gudmundsson) requires iteration but is more accurate The statistical curves have the form We/W0 = A·W0C, so the unknown appears on both sides.
  • The equation can always be solved explicitly without iteration

How is the total mission fuel fraction determined?

  • As the product of the individual mission segment mass fractions mF/mMTO = 1 − Π(mi/mi−1) over all segments.
  • Start, warm-up, taxi, take-off, climb, descent, landing and shutdown are taken from tables Typical values for transport jets: 0.990 for start/warm-up, 0.990 taxi, 0.995 take-off, 0.980 climb, 0.990 descent, 0.992 landing/taxi/shutdown.
  • Cruise segments are computed with the Breguet range equation Loiter segments with the Breguet endurance equation.
  • For battery-electric aircraft the segment fractions are multiplied in the same way
The table values are based on experience and judgement — there is no substitute for common sense.

Which statements about the Breguet factors used in the lecture are correct?

m1/m0 = e−s/Bs  and  m1/m0 = e−t/Bt

  • The Breguet range factor for level cruise is Bs = (L/D)·V/SFCT It has the dimension of a length — typically km.
  • The Breguet endurance factor for level cruise is Bt = (L/D)/SFCT It has the dimension of a time — typically h. The speed drops out.
  • For climb and descent the flight path angle enters, via a term with sinγ The factor becomes smaller in a climb, because part of the fuel energy goes into potential energy.
  • The exponent must be non-dimensional, so units have to be watched carefully Typical consistent choice: [s] = km, [Bs] = km, [SFCT] = lb/lb/h = 1/h, [V] = km/h.
  • Doubling the fuel mass doubles the range

Which typical thrust-specific fuel consumption values apply to subsonic jets?

  • High-bypass turbofan: ≈ 0.5 (cruise) / 0.4 (loiter) [1/h] The best of the three — the reason every transport aircraft uses one.
  • Low-bypass turbofan: ≈ 0.8 (cruise) / 0.7 (loiter) [1/h] Typical for older or military aircraft.
  • Pure turbojet: ≈ 0.9 (cruise) / 0.8 (loiter) [1/h] The worst of the three.
  • Loiter values are lower than cruise values Consistent across all engine types.
  • Propeller SFC values can be inserted into the jet equations unchanged

What has to be treated differently when sizing a battery-electric aircraft?

  • Electric motors do not lose power with altitude, unlike combustion engines The power lapse models (density ratio, Gagg and Ferrar) do not apply.
  • The cooling performance of the heat exchanger still falls with the density ratio So an oversized cooling system may be needed, and its extra drag must be accounted for in the aerodynamic performance.
  • The segment battery mass fractions are summed, not multiplied Unlike fuel, a battery does not get lighter as it is discharged — the aircraft mass stays constant.
  • The battery fraction depends on the specific energy E* of the battery Together with the total system efficiency from battery to shaft, the propeller efficiency and L/D.
  • The matching diagram cannot be used for electric aircraft

The system efficiency chain: battery 0.97–0.99, inverter 0.95–0.99, e-motor 0.94–0.99, gearbox 0.98–1.00 — multiplied together.

Which "lessons learned" does the lecture draw from preliminary sizing?

  • Preliminary sizing fixes take-off mass, thrust or power, and wing area The three parameters that determine the whole rest of the design.
  • The calculated values are only valid for the first "shot" at the design They are subject to change in the next design stages — treat them very carefully and do not regard them as final.
  • A poor CD0 estimate can threaten the whole design It is singled out explicitly in the lessons learned. Both drag and mass errors propagate through the entire sizing loop.
  • You must be sure about the mission profile before sizing Changes further along the timeline may force a resizing and become extremely expensive.
  • Statistical values from textbooks can be applied without further thought

The better the initial estimates, the less re-designing has to be done.

From whose point of view can an aircraft design be assessed?

  • The aircraft manufacturer and the supplier For both, the most important requirement is to generate a profit.
  • The operator, i.e. the airline or the private operator Commercial operators also assess by profit; direct operating cost is their key figure.
  • The passenger, the certification authority and the population on the ground Comfort, compliance and noise — three completely different sets of criteria applied to the same aircraft.
  • A design assessment is a purely technical evaluation of flight performance

When is the development of a new aircraft considered profitable for a manufacturer?

  • When the return on investment is high The capital tied up in the development has to earn more than it would elsewhere.
  • When the break-even point is reached early Aircraft programmes run for decades; the later the break-even, the higher the risk that the market has moved on before it arrives.
  • To estimate the profit, revenues and cost have to be compared Different methods for estimating both can be found in the literature.
  • A high selling price per aircraft alone makes a programme profitable
