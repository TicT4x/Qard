Give the FAR-25 definition of a Powerplant and sketch it. Which components connect Powerplant and Airframe?

Definition (FAR 25)

Powerplant: all the equipment, including structural installations, that constitute a unit power source. That is, all equipment including the structural installations that form one self-contained power source.

Propulsion System = Powerplant in the sense of the lecture

  • Dressed Engine (CS-E certified)
  • Inlet / Intake
  • Nacelle (inlet cowl, fan cowl doors, apron/fixed cowl, core cowl)
  • Mixer or nozzle
  • Thrust Reverser Unit (TRU)
  • Buyer Furnished Equipment (BFE)
  • Mounts

Connection to the Airframe

  • Front mount and rear mount (with thrust struts if applicable) — transfer of thrust, weight and moments
  • Pylon — by definition part of the airframe, and the actual interface
  • Service trays in the pylon: fuel, hydraulics, bleed air, oil, electrical lines, control/monitoring lines
  • Drains and vents, firewall / apron

What is a Dressed Engine?

The engine with all installations required for operation — as the Engine Supplier delivers it, certified to CS-E.

Two points of view

  • Engine Supplier: the engine with all installations required for operation.
  • Customer: the product as delivered. Since the Airframer may no longer modify the CS-E certified engine, all parts that are not accessible after assembly are included, as are brackets and harnesses mounted on the engine.

Distinction

  • Dressings = connections (pipes, tubes, wires) on the dressed engine to accessories and controls.
  • Externals = all connections added to the dressed engine — they belong to the EBU (Engine Build Unit).
Rule of thumb: bare engine → dressed engine (CS-E) → + EBU/externals + nacelle + TRU + BFE + mounts = Propulsion System.

Define Buyer Furnished Equipment (BFE).

Equipment supplied by the customer (Airframer/airline) that has to be integrated into the engine by the Engine Supplier — it is designed by the Airframer but mounted on the engine. BFE is therefore a classic interface case (partly certified with the dressed engine, partly with powerplant/airframe).

Typical BFE components

  • Components of the high pressure bleed system (precooler, bleed valves)
  • Hydraulic system / hydraulic pump
  • Aircraft generator (IDG)
  • Starter system
  • Engine vibration monitoring system, speed probes
  • Engine mount system

Most of the BFE sits on the accessory gearbox — which is why the AGB is the governing hardpoint for the nacelle diameter.

Define LRU. Why are LRUs important for the operator, and which parties influence the maintainability analysis in “Design for Service”?

Definition

Line Replaceable Unit: an engine mounted accessory that can be readily changed with the engine on wing during line maintenance. An assembly which forms part of a system, designed to be removed and replaced in the event of failure to improve maintainability.

Why important?

  • Line maintenance cost is the part of maintenance cost that can still be influenced during design — hardly at all after design freeze.
  • Short MTTR and low MMH/MA → fewer unscheduled ground times, delays and cancellations (AOG cost, loss of reputation).
  • Maintenance accounts for ~31 % of the engine-related DOC (≈ 8 % of total DOC).
  • No special tooling / no highly qualified personnel required → lower cost.

Typical LRU items

Filter units (oil/fuel filters), TRU components, fuel metering unit, oil pump, fuel pump, PMA/alternator, IDG/generator, ignition unit, EEC, sensors and speed probes, bleed valves.

Parties involved

  • Engine Supplier (ES) — design engineer, service engineer, DMU group, maintenance group
  • Airframer (A) — defines installation environment, nacelle, accessibility
  • Airline / operator (customer) — defines in the CRS what is an LRU and demands MTBCD values and change times
  • Subcontractors / BFE suppliers, certification authority

Which items are LRUs is decided by the customer based on experience, statistics and life aspects.

Name three tasks of the pylon.

Definition

Connecting interface between powerplant and airframe (wing or fuselage). By definition the pylon belongs to the airframe.

Tasks

  1. Mechanical attachment and load transfer: weight, thrust, aerodynamic forces on the nacelle and derived moments (gyroscopic moments of rotating parts) into the wing or fuselage structure.
  2. Vibration damping / decoupling: the pylon must be stiff enough, but with a natural frequency far away from that of the wing — coupling with identical resonance frequencies is not acceptable (flutter).
  3. Housing of the service trays: fuel, hydraulics, bleed air, generator cables, control and monitoring lines — separated into zones (firewalls).

Further tasks worth mentioning

  • Rated break point: under overload (belly landing, ditching) the engine–pylon connection must fail before the pylon–wing connection breaks → the integral tanks stay sealed.
  • Fire protection: apron as firewall, integrated extinguishing system (Freon bottles).
  • Aerodynamic fairing (upper/side fairings only carry air loads).

Explain the nacelle geometry: intake, forebody, midbody, afterbody, core cowl, apron, cowl doors.

  • Intake: the three-dimensional surface extending internally from the highlight (leading edge) to the fan face or compressor casing.
  • Forebody / nosecowl: external surface from the intake highlight to the midbody / fan cowl.
  • Midbody / fan cowl: classically the cylindrical region from the end of the forebody to the start of the contraction; on nacelles with continuous curvature it runs from forebody to afterbody.
  • Afterbody: from the end of the midbody to the nozzle or fan nozzle exit.
  • Core cowl: surface around the hot core duct, downstream of the fan nozzle, washed by the fan flow.
  • Apron (fixed cowl): the fixed part of the engine cowling (also firewall towards the pylon).
  • Cowl doors: the part of the cowling that can be opened for maintenance.
  • Bypass duct: duct downstream of the fan stage and outside the core, leading to the mixer or fan nozzle.
  • Exhaust cone: guides the core flow back to the centre line with low losses; its size follows from the turbine diameter.

What are GSE and service tray? What is the difference between tube, pipe and drain?

  • GSE (Ground Support Equipment): all support equipment needed for propulsion operation and maintenance — to inspect, test, adjust, transfer data, calibrate, measure, assemble, disassemble, handle, transport, maintain, repair and overhaul.
  • Service tray: aerodynamically faired duct (NACA profile) that routes supply lines through the bypass flow to the core engine: fuel, bleed air (thickest line), VGV actuation, fuel shut-off, control/monitoring lines. At least one is required; a second one should be avoided (strong drag increase) unless pipe weight argues against it.
  • Tube: a hollow cylinder that conveys objects, liquids or gases, or functions as a passage.
  • Pipe: a long tube made of metal or plastic used to carry a fluid or gas.
  • Drain: tube inserted into a cavity to remove unwanted material.

Which reviews (gates) exist in the design process, and why are they important?

The reviews

  • Concept Review (CR): after selection of a single technical concept. Confirms that it is the optimum solution and that the technical risk is acceptable. Afterwards formal offers are made to customers.
  • Preliminary Design Review (PDR): after analytical and experimental conformance of concept and supply chain. Confirms that the risk is acceptable for full program launch. Customer commitment follows.
  • Critical Design Review (CDR): once the detailed product and manufacturing definition is largely complete. Confirms that product and supply chain, including verification/validation plans, meet the requirements at acceptable risk.
  • Installation Readiness Review (IRR): before delivery of the hardware for first installation. Confirms that all regulatory requirements are met and that the product is ready to be installed.

Why important?

  • At each gate an independent review panel assesses program status, deliverables and technical as well as business risk — and only then releases the program to continue.
  • Errors are found early: the further the design has progressed, the more expensive changes become.
  • They synchronize Engine Supplier and Airframer (interface management) and prevent misplanning.
  • The maintainability demonstrations class I / II / III are tied to CR / PDR / CDR.

Define the RFP (Request for Proposal).

The Request for Proposal is the document with which the customer (Airframer or airline) defines the intended operation and therefore the requirements on the engine, and requests a design proposal. It essentially describes the characteristics and performance of aircraft and engine.

Typical content

  • Max. range, max. altitude, max. speed, cruise speed
  • Max. take-off thrust, weight
  • Safety and reliability requirements
  • Basic philosophy, e.g.:
    • simple and low cost → no complicated geometries, efficiency/weight not optimal (typical short range)
    • highest technology level → optimum but expensive engine (typical long range)

It is issued at stage 1 entry; the ability to respond to it is the exit criterion of stage 0.

Define the CRS (Customer Requirements Specification).

Contractual document in which the expected characteristics of the engine are laid down. It is created jointly by Engine Supplier and customer in the course of concluding the contract.

Important: the CRS shall cover the requirements but not prescribe a solution.

Content

  • Size, thrust, weight (guarantee weight) of the engine
  • Geometric baseline: horizontal/vertical/lateral position, engine attitude (toe-in/toe-out, droop angle), mount and strut geometries, nacelle weight requirements
  • Operational requirements: flight envelope, attitude envelope, ground/crosswind requirements, relight and starting envelope, installed performance guarantees (thrust, SFC), reverse thrust
  • Responsibilities of Airframer and Engine Supplier
  • Maintenance aspects: accessibility, arrangement, removal/installation, LRU list with MTBCD and change times, LH/RH commonality, DMU demonstrations
  • Applicable documents, delivery details, engine control & operation, drains, fuel and oil system, flight test support
  • Certification requirements may be included by the Airframer

Describe three possibilities to evaluate an aircraft's mission profile.

  1. Evaluation of the seat–range diagram. Plot range over number of seats of existing types and look for market niches (voids). Questions: technical or economic cause of the void? Can it be opened up by clever design or new technology? Which city pair fits? How many passengers, at what ticket price?
  2. Analysis of airline route networks. A network segment (regional / international / intercontinental) is characterized by the average passenger demand and the average stage length — together they give the reference mission. Load factor and range flexibility follow from this.
  3. Trade-off studies. Forecasts based on extensive computational models: demand for new types of a certain size/range, replacement of existing concepts or a completely new concept. They account for airport infrastructure (e.g. runway length for the A380), fleet policy and political boundary conditions.

Best practice is to combine all three; the result is summarized in the flight mission specification (mission profile, flight envelope, load profile).

Define seat load factor and range flexibility.

Load factor

Load Factor = Sold Capacity / Offered Capacity

Ratio of sold to offered capacity. It increases with range: long range aircraft typically have a higher load factor than short range aircraft.

Range flexibility

Range Flexibility = Range / Average Flight Distance

Ratio of maximum range to average stage length flown. The design range is deliberately larger than the average stage length because operators expect flexibility in their route network. Range flexibility also increases with the range of the type.

Name Certification Specifications relevant for engine integration. What is certified to CS-25, what to CS-E?

Regulations

  • CS-25 / FAR 25 — Large Aeroplanes (“transport category”): the aircraft including nacelle, TRU, EBU, BFE, pylon.
  • CS-E / FAR 33 — Engines: the dressed engine (including mounts).
  • CS-P — propellers; CS-34 emissions; CS-36 / FAR 36 noise; CS-23 / FAR 23 smaller types and commuters.
  • ICAO — recommendations on emissions and noise; ACARE goals (partly not yet mandatory).
  • Authority in Europe: EASA (since Nov. 2004), executive tasks on national level (LBA in Germany).

Typical compliance items with integration relevance

  • Anti-icing, engine burst / rotor burst, OEI, bird strike, hail/rain ingestion
  • Fan-blade-off (no object may leave the fan casing), cross-engine debris
  • Ventilation against accumulation of flammable fluids and vapours; fireproof fan cowl doors; apron as firewall

  • Ground clearance: min. 18 inches for the nacelle at maximum load; the fan must not touch down if the nose gear collapses

Mandatory tests before certification

150 h endurance run, fan-blade-off, bird strike, rain/hail ingestion, altitude test, icing test, strain gauge and rotor integrity test, cold start, crosswind test, cyclic test, noise test, rig testing, flight test.

Why are there no single-engine transport aircraft? What does the choice of the number of engines depend on?

Why at least two

  • The certification requirements demand at least two engines for aircraft with more than 10 occupants (FAR 23 and FAR 25).
  • The reason is redundancy: after reaching v₁ the take-off must be continuable even with one engine failed, including the 35 ft screen height.
  • Without redundancy an engine failure on a long range flight or during take-off would be immediately fatal.

What does the number depend on?

  • Required total thrust or MTOW: T_TO = k_TO · MTOW with k_TO ≈ 0.25…0.33 N/kg
  • Available engine classes (thrust categories offered by the manufacturers)
  • Range and route network → ETOPS boundary conditions
  • Permissible fan diameter (ground clearance, landing gear length, interference)
  • OEI yawing moment and the resulting vertical tail size
  • DOC: weight, maintenance effort, spare part stock
  • Wing structure (bending moment relief) and flutter behaviour

Trijets appeared in the early 1960s as an answer to ETOPS. Because of the demanding integration of the centre engine (S-duct or tail installation: intake losses, distortion, large hole in the fuselage structure, reverser issues) they have practically disappeared today.

Explain ETOPS and its effect on aircraft design in the past. Which technologies and operational methods are necessary?

Definition

ETOPS = Extended-range Twin-engine Operational Performance Standards. The rule defines the time in minutes within which a twin-engine transport aircraft must be able to reach a suitable diversion airport after an in-flight shut-down (IFSD).

Historical development and effect on design

  • 1953, FAA: twinjets must be able to land within 60 min with one engine failed — which practically closed the North Atlantic and the Pacific.
  • Consequence: three- and four-engine types were built for long range (DC-10, L-1011, B747). The trijet exists only because of ETOPS.
  • With more reliable jet engines the rule was relaxed: 90 min (1974) → 180 min (1987) → 207 min (1997).
  • Consequence: twinjets take over the long range (B767, B777, A330) — trijets and quadjets lose importance. Today largely superseded by LROPS (Long Range Operational Performance Standards), which regulate equipment, safety installations and reliability for other types as well.

Compliance criterion

The basis is the IFSD rate, i.e. the average number of engine shut-downs per flight hour for the combination of airframe and engine. Required is evidence of fewer than 0.02 IFSD per 1000 flight hours over a life of 250,000 flight hours.

ETOPS has no relation to water or distance over water — it simply limits the permissible routings.

Necessary technologies and measures

  • Very reliable engines; engine condition monitoring (ECM), predictive/on-condition maintenance
  • Redundant systems (electrics, hydraulics, bleed), APU in-flight start capability, sufficient battery time
  • Fire protection in cargo and engine zones with sufficient extinguishing agent duration
  • Nacelle designed for windmilling drag (nacelle design is ETOPS relevant)
  • Operational: approved diversion airports along the route with weather and operating minima, special crew and dispatch procedures, maintenance programme and part tracking (“ETOPS parts”), no simultaneous work on both engines

Performance background

In cruise only about 80 % of the available thrust is used. If one of two engines fails and the other runs at full power, the loss compared with cruise thrust is only about 30 % — but the optimum cruise altitude and speed can no longer be held (drift down, increased fuel consumption).

Explain the family concept (common core) in engine design.

Because developing a new engine is expensive, lengthy (7–10 years up to certification) and risky, an existing core engine is used for several applications and adapted to different thrust requirements (“minimum change concept”).

Three levels of change

  1. Common core: geometries stay unchanged; turbine entry/exit temperature and blading are adapted.
  2. Scaling: in addition, change of geometries and of the primary mass flow.
  3. Common technology: in addition, change of the number of stages (configuration).

Advantages

  • Considerably less design effort → lower cost, shorter development time, lower risk
  • Experience and improvements can be transferred to existing engines of the family
  • Similar maintenance procedures within a family → commonality, fewer spares and less training

Prerequisite: when designing the core, flexibility must be considered from the very beginning — already during preliminary sizing, trade-off studies are run for possible variants and their operating behaviour. Example: the RB211 family (Rolls-Royce).

Which design concepts (combinations of airframe and propulsion) exist?

  1. Existing airframe / existing propulsion system — the effort depends solely on the compatibility of the two systems.
  2. Existing airframe / new propulsion system — successful types are continuously developed further: more range, more payload, shorter runway, less fuel consumption, less noise (re-engining).
  3. New airframe / new propulsion system — new engines have to meet increased requirements (safety, reliability, fuel consumption, emissions); often new technology and materials → higher price.
  4. New airframe / family concept propulsion system — the core of an existing engine is scaled (common core / scaling / common technology).

Existing aero engines offer only limited room for adaptation; design and certification of a new engine take 7–10 years on average.

Sketch the thermodynamic cycle of a bypass engine and explain what follows from it for the integration.

Stations and trends

  • Intake: diffusion of the flow, pressure rise (ram), velocity drops. Key figure: intake pressure recovery.
  • Fan / LPC: pressure rises, flow splits into core and bypass stream; the fan produces the largest share of thrust and absorbs the largest part of the turbine power.
  • HPC: strong rise in pressure and temperature; the annulus area contracts (rising hub line or falling casing line) so that the axial velocity stays roughly constant.
  • Combustor: heat addition at approximately constant pressure, temperature maximum (TET).
  • HPT/LPT: expansion, pressure and temperature drop, power for compressor and fan.
  • Nozzle: acceleration, conversion of the remaining pressure drop into thrust.

What follows for the integration?

  • The distribution of pressure, temperature and velocity determines the choice of materials and the cooling demand.
  • Hot regions are barred for accessories → thermal keep-out-zone around the combustor.
  • Air off-takes are placed where the flow velocity is low and the loss is smallest — at the lowest possible compressor stage.
  • Modules are coupled through the process conditions: exit condition of one module = entry condition of the next; components on one shaft (HPC/HPT) have the same speed → power balance.
  • The cycle is the prerequisite for the detailed weight estimation.

Which engine types exist and how is the selection linked to propulsive efficiency? What is the thrust rating curve?

Selection

It follows from flight speed, altitude and range, plus installed weight and the FAR 36 noise regulations. The type chosen is the one with the best propulsive efficiency for the mission (= lowest SFC). Propulsive efficiency is the ratio of thrust · flight speed to the energy provided by the engine and depends on the ratio c_flight/c_jet.

TypeApplicationRemark
Piston enginelow speed and altitude75–300 kW, economic limit
Turbopropup to about 600 km/hhigh mass flow, small acceleration of the air → very high propulsive efficiency; above that a drop due to blade tip Mach number; acceptance problem with passengers

TurbofanMa 0.6–0.9, standard of commercial aviationcompromise: high secondary flow, lower jet velocity → better efficiency, less noise, better SFC; plus forced mixer and acoustic liner

Thrust rating curve

Up to the critical ambient temperature (“kink point”, flat rating) the maximum thrust is constant. Above it the available thrust drops because the turbine entry temperature is held constant to protect engine life. Important ratings: max. take-off, max. continuous, max. climb, max. cruise.

Rough estimate: T_TO = k_TO · MTOW with k_TO ≈ 0.25 … 0.33 N/kg.

Describe the three main maintenance principles.

  1. Preventive maintenance — the aim is to avoid failures: periodic replacement of life-limited parts and condition checks within the time between overhaul (TBO). Includes scheduled, line, light and heavy maintenance.
  2. Predictive maintenance — requires continuous monitoring of the propulsion system condition (engine condition monitoring, ECM) in order to detect performance deterioration and predict the maintenance demand. It allows plannable maintenance and thus a maintenance free operating period; it corresponds to on-condition maintenance. Side effect: fuel savings, because the optimum operating condition is restored early.
  3. Corrective maintenance — unscheduled action after a failure has occurred: defect analysis, failure recording, rectification. It should be avoided, because unscheduled ground time causes high losses. Fast fault detection and isolation reduce the time.
Overhaul is not one of these three principles but maintenance level 3.

What are the primary objectives of aircraft maintenance?

  • Maximum safety of flight operation
  • Reliability — avoidance of IFSD and aborted take-off
  • Economy of operation
  • Reduction of unscheduled ground times and delays, which affect passenger comfort and the airline's image
  • Fulfilment of the “instructions for continued airworthiness”

Already in the technical design one therefore considers the failure probability, the avoidance of in-flight shut-downs and the operational effect of a malfunction. Decisive for the quick restoration of serviceability is the fast identification and fast replacement of failed components — which is why the design process puts a focus on maintainability, a substantial part of the DOC.

Describe the kinds of Keep-Out-Zones (KOZ).

KOZs reserve, inside the digital mock-up, the space that will be needed later — they are 3D bodies and must not overlap any solid (that would be a clash).

1. Design (occupied zone — no other model inside)

  • Installed position of components or tools
  • Placeholder for parts not yet designed
  • Assembly or removal path

2. Kinematics

  • Space for movement or vibration of a part: hold-open rods, VGV mechanism, vibration-mounted units
  • Inspection areas, e.g. borescope test
  • Exceptions possible for synchronized mechanisms (e.g. variable guide vanes)

3. Maintenance

  • On-wing service: minimum space for maintenance, tool/hand access, GSE (oil replenishment, overflow, draining)
  • Accessibility: access panels, tool movement
  • Removal / installation: removal space for LRUs, filters etc.
  • Visual path: line of sight to the oil sight glass, name plates

4. Safety

  • Ventilation of units (vent air around accessories)
  • Heat zone, e.g. combustor
  • Burst zones: fan-blade-off, turbine disk burst (fan case KOZ, disk burst KOZ)
The exam often asks for “3 kinds” — name design, kinematics, maintenance and safety; this is complete and covers every way of counting.

What is “Design for Service”? Name and briefly describe 8 aspects.

Design for Service (= design for line maintenance) means that maintainability is shaped during design, because after design freeze there is hardly any influence left on maintenance cost. The goals are safety, reliability and economy with minimum unscheduled ground times.

The eight aspects

  1. Consider the three maintenance levels (line / shop / overhaul) — level 1 tasks must be possible on-wing: cleaning, servicing, visual check, simple repair.
  2. LRU concept — design units so that they can be changed quickly with the engine on wing; which parts are LRUs is defined by the customer in the CRS (with MTBCD and change time).

Describe the Design for Service process, in particular for LRUs.

Basic loop

  1. Analyse the requirements and break them down to component level (breakdown of the product into subcomponents).
  2. Design at every level, respecting KOZ, accessibility and removal path.
  3. Verification at every level, until the complete unit is finished and compliance with the requirements is demonstrated.
  4. Reviews (CR, PDR, CDR) with the associated maintainability demonstrations class I / II / III.

  • Check removal KOZ
  • Check the complete removal path by short animation — dynamic clash investigation shows all collisions and violations of clearances

Explain collision management (level 1–3) and the definition of a “clash”.

Definitions

The interface area is the contact zone or the region of small clearances. A clash is either a hard collision or a violation of a defined minimum clearance. Note: the definition of the clearance may differ between Engine Supplier and Airframer. Clashes and small clearances have to be investigated in a report.

Levels

  • Level 3: collisions within one design team — entirely in its own responsibility. As a principle, a clash-free DMU model shall be delivered.
  • Level 2: collisions between component groups of different teams within one company.
  • Level 1: collisions between design teams of different companies.

After collision detection, changes are initiated; it is useful to distinguish minor (without review) and major (subject to approval). Example from the script: a mount bolt clashes with an oil pipe.

How does the weight build-up method work, and which precondition must be met for the detailed weight estimation?

Precondition

The thermodynamic cycle calculation must be available — only from it follow mass flows, pressures, temperatures and thus annulus areas, number of stages and blade geometries.

Weight build-up

  1. Sections — split the engine into modules (fan, LPC, HPC, combustor, HPT, LPT, casings, externals)
  2. Stages — split each module into stages
  3. Breakdown of each stage into rotor/stator blades, casing, disks and additional hardware
  4. From the 2D blade geometry the volume is derived and, with the material density, the mass of every blade; likewise disk design and clearances between rotor and stator
  5. Summing up from the smallest part to the complete module → precise total weight

The estimation additionally provides the centre of gravity with an accuracy of about 0.5 inch — important for the design of the mounts and for the overall aircraft CG.

How is the interface index (IFN) defined, and which maturity states exist?

The very first step of the process. The number contains the most relevant properties of the interface for easy recognition and identifies the two interface partners: each partner declares his interface with the same system — matching numbers show who is involved.

Explain the function and arrangement of the accessory gearbox. What do “wet side” and “dry side” mean?

Function

The gearboxes provide mechanical power for engine and aircraft accessories.

  • Internal gearbox in the core engine: starts the engine by turning the HP rotor; densely packed with fast rotating parts → effective oil cooling required.
  • Accessory gearbox (AGB) on the intermediate casing (IMC), driven via the radial drive shaft (RDS): provides rotational power for accessories and starter and offers a mounting face for every unit.

Typical consumers

Engine side: starter, fuel pump, oil pump, alternator (PMA for the EEC), breather. Aircraft side: generator/IDG, hydraulic pump.

Arrangement

  • Crescent shape — aerodynamic optimum for drag reduction.
  • At the bottom of the engine — good maintainability and outside the fan-blade-off danger zone; it can also be mounted at the side for more ground clearance (CFM56 of the B737 compared with the A320).
  • The AGB is one of the largest installations and, together with the IDG, largely governs the nacelle diameter — larger generators push the aerolines outwards and increase drag and weight.

Wet side / dry side

Electrical units sit on the dry side, fluid-filled units on the wet side, separated by the starter gear shaft. Reason: separating electrics and hot air ducts (risk of ignition) from fluid-filled units minimizes the fire risk. Typically the generator and the PMA sit on the dry side — neither is vital for engine operation.

Safety detail

The drive shafts of the accessories have a designed breaking point (shear neck) so that a seized unit cannot block the RDS. Units vital for engine operation have no rated break point — their failure requires immediate shut-down. The air turbine starter usually delivers the highest torque and is therefore the design driver; it sits with the shortest drive train to the core.

Which two main constraints determine the arrangement of the accessories? What is cross-engine debris?

The two main constraints

  1. Cross-engine debris (FAR 25) — debris from a failed engine must not hit units vital for the other engine. Because of the assumed “infinite kinetic energy” of a bursting disk the case cannot be excluded, but the risk can be minimized: hydraulic pump, oil pump and fuel pump are arranged so that damage and engine failure due to foreign object damage or a disk burst on the opposite engine is most unlikely.
  2. Fan-blade-off deflection envelope (CS-E) — when a blade is lost the fan casing deforms; arranging the AGB inside this danger zone would lead to consequential damage. Choice of fan casing material: a “soft” material of high strength and low deformation (e.g. Kevlar) → high weight; a “hard” material of low strength and high deformation (e.g. titanium) → larger keep-out area.

Further arrangement rules

  • Separate electrical units and hot air ducts from fluid-filled units (fire risk) — wet/dry side.
  • Air installations (air turbine starter, anti-icing) consist of large pipes and require a lot of space.
  • Maintenance of one component must not affect neighbouring parts; no removal of other systems for access; provide a removal path for every component.
  • Rotator provision for turning the engine by hand for inspections.

What is bleed air needed for — internally and externally?

Internal air system

Part of the compressed air does not contribute directly to thrust but is necessary for safe and efficient operation:

  • Cooling — above all combustor and turbines (highest heat loads); also accessories such as the generator, which reject considerable heat.
  • Sealing — minimize leakage losses and above all prevent oil escaping. Oil outside the bearing chamber can cause an engine fire, oil in the main stream can cause cabin odour or visible smoke.
  • Bearing load control (axial thrust balancing).
  • Compressor stabilization: dumping part of the air into the bypass — however with a performance loss, which is why variable guide vanes (VIGV/VSV) are preferable.
  • Anti-icing.

External air system (customer bleeds)

Considerable amounts are bled for aircraft systems: cabin pressurization, cabin heating, water pressure system, airframe de-icing. Even though they are routed externally, they affect the internal system because they shift the compressor operating point.

Design goal

The most robust architecture while meeting the customer requirements, with a large operating envelope, fault tolerance and risk minimization — bleeding from the lowest possible compressor stage and minimizing leakage.

Describe the fuel, oil and control systems and their integration problems.

Fuel system

  • Priority: uninterrupted fuel supply at constant pressure, governed by thrust management.
  • FOHE (fuel oil heat exchanger): cools the oil and preheats the fuel.
  • FMU (fuel metering unit): meters the fuel as commanded by the EEC and additionally actuates the VIGVs of the HPC.
  • FFT (fuel flow transmitter): signal proportional to the mass flow for monitoring.
  • Integration problem: parts of the fuel system are designed by the Airframer, others by the Engine Supplier — both must fit together perfectly (interface management).

Oil system

  • Tasks: lubrication, cooling, corrosion protection.
  • Oil cooling via FOHE or SACOC (surface air cooled oil cooler) in the bypass flow — its arrangement must produce minimum flow loss and must not block any access panels.
  • Breather: driven at high speed by the gearbox, it centrifuges oil out of the oil-laden sealing air and returns it.

Electronics and control

  • FADEC (full authority digital engine control) — designed by the Airframer; it removes workload from the pilot, monitors and controls nearly all quantities and keeps the engine safely within its limits.
  • Components: EEC, ignition system and starter control, actuation, communication with aircraft systems, fail-safe control, fuel pumps and metering, bleed valves, power supply, sensors and health monitoring, PMA (generates the power for engine operation → self-sustaining).
  • CMCS (central maintenance computer system): monitors the integrity of the aircraft systems, collects and displays information on failed components, stores a fault history.
  • ACMS (aircraft condition monitoring system, optional): on-board recording for later performance and trend analyses, snapshot recording, alert reports.

Which requirements and constraints apply to tubing practice?

The design of pipes and tubes is one of the last but most difficult steps. The integration team defines all surfaces and diameters for pipes, casing envelopes and unit positions and makes sure that all dressings and externals can be installed sensibly — including assembly tolerances, thermal expansion and relative movements within powerplant and pylon.

What pipes are needed for

  • Fuel: high and low pressure lines, drainage, venting
  • Oil: high pressure, scavenge, drainage
  • Bleed air: different velocities, pressures, temperatures
  • Control and monitoring: speed, pressure, temperature, fuel metering, pumps
  • Drainage of leakage and condensate
  • Ventilation of the danger zones so that smoke and easily flammable gases do not accumulate

Constraints

  • Steady pressure, temperature (max./min.), velocity and mass flow of the fluid
  • Environmental conditions: temperature, pressure, abrasion, corrosion, FOD, fire, cleaning agents, chemical properties of the fluid
  • Vibrations — self-excited and externally excited; place brackets so that no resonance occurs
  • Sufficient flexibility and accuracy to avoid excessive forces during installation
  • Consider thermal expansion/contraction

Rigid metal pipes are very stiff at large diameters; manufacturing tolerances can create high stresses in the pipe and in the connected parts. Flexible pipes are usually heavier, thicker and more expensive; non-metallic materials are often unsuitable because of the high engine temperatures.

Explain drains and vents: what is their task, where are they located and why exactly there?

Task

  • Drains remove fluids from the danger and fire zones: condensate, hydraulic fluid, oil and fuel leakage. In this way nothing flammable accumulates.
  • Vents ventilate the danger zones so that no flammable fluids, vapours or smoke can accumulate — also a certification requirement on the nacelle design.

Where

On nacelle and pylon, in the lower region, on both sides of the bottom centerline.

Why exactly there — the reasoning

The pressure distribution on the nacelle has its maximum at the stagnation point on the intake lip and drops towards the rear. On the midbody the pressure drops particularly quickly on both sides of the bottom centerline. Exactly this region is favourable as drain position, because the local pressure distribution ensures that the fluid is carried away strictly downstream and re-ingestion through unfavourable flow conditions is excluded.

Further points

  • The division into sections allows fast diagnosis of where a leak is located.
  • Vents and drains are part of the FICD (functional interface control document).
  • In addition, the nacelle must avoid re-ingestion of drained fluids, have fireproof fan cowl doors in the accessory zone and the apron as firewall towards the pylon.

What is induced drag, and why does it play only a minor role in nacelle design?

What it is

Induced drag is the unavoidable price of generating lift (tip vortices, downwash). It can therefore be understood as an aerodynamic loss caused by the weight of the aircraft: more weight → more lift required → more loss. It changes continuously over the mission as fuel is burned.

Why it is secondary for the nacelle

  • Chargeability by weight share: induced drag is charged to the components in proportion to their weight share of the total aircraft. The nacelle only has a small share of it.
  • The nacelle does not itself produce noteworthy lift — on the contrary, an explicit design goal is to avoid lift on the nacelle and to optimize its attitude. It is not a lift generating body like the wing.
  • For the nacelle, friction drag (wetted area), pressure/spillage drag, wave drag and interference drag dominate instead.
Note the special cases in which the nacelle does produce induced drag: the overwing installation — there the nacelle acts as a low aspect ratio wing ahead of the wing and creates a large lift-dependent drag. Likewise the spanwise position influences the lift distribution of the wing and therefore the induced drag of the aircraft.

What is interference drag? How is it defined, booked and minimized?

Definition

Interference drag arises from the mutual disturbance of the flow fields of two bodies. Two components, each with its own wetted area, together produce more drag than the sum of their individual drags:

D_interference = D₁₊₂ − (D₁ + D₂)

Bookkeeping

The attribution is arbitrary, because the source cannot be determined unambiguously and undisputedly. In practice it is charged to the body with the largest “room to move” — the one that can most easily be changed in order to reduce the interference drag.

Physics

  • The closer two components sit together, the higher the interference drag.
  • Acceleration and deceleration in the gully flow (channel between nacelle, pylon, wing and fuselage) affect the boundary layer and the drag of the bodies.
  • Critical are the locations of maximum relative thickness; three-dimensional boundary layers develop in the corners pylon–wing and pylon–nacelle.

Design rules for minimization

  • Keep sufficient distance; place closely coupled engines so that the trailing edge lies upstream of the wing leading edge.
  • Do not put the locations of maximum thickness of nacelle and pylon at the same axial position.
  • Mach number in the gully flow not above 1.1; observe entry contraction and exit diffusion as external boundary conditions.
  • Align toe-in and droop angle with the local flow; avoid lift generation on the nacelle.
  • The pylon leading edge should undercut the wing leading edge (lie behind the stagnation point).
  • Start fuselage contouring (area ruling) sufficiently far upstream of the nacelle; fairings at wing–fuselage junctions.
  • Keep the interference with the empennage small.

Explain thrust and drag bookkeeping as well as the difference between uninstalled and installed thrust.

Bookkeeping rule

  • Losses inside the captured stream tube and inside the fan jet boundary are booked as thrust.
  • Forces outside this boundary are booked as drag.
  • Resulting forces in the exhaust jet due to wing or external flow influence are booked as drag.
  • Friction drag of the nacelle is by definition aircraft drag.

Why it matters

Firstly for the aerodynamic efficiency of aircraft and engine, secondly for meeting the Airframer's thrust requirements. On the test bed, without the airframe, the Engine Supplier measures the uninstalled thrust; for the Airframer the installed thrust counts.

Definitions

  • Uninstalled thrust: total axial thrust of an isolated engine under ideal flow conditions; it contains neither pressure nor friction losses of the external nacelle flow, since these are charged to the aircraft.
  • Net standard thrust: momentum flux between “infinitely far upstream” and the exit plane, including the pre-entry force:
    X_N = (p_e − p_∞)·A_e + ρ_e·v_e²·A_e − ρ_∞·v_∞²·A_∞
  • Installed thrust: net thrust minus drag and losses in the external flow: nacelle (forebody, midbody, afterbody), pylon and interferences. Harder to determine, but decisive for the flight performance. For flight measurements the results have to be standardized with ISA reference values.
Curious but important: looking only at the external surface forces (“without thrust correction”), the installed configuration has less drag than the clean one.

Why does drag rise disproportionately at high subsonic Mach numbers, and what does a supercritical aerofoil achieve?

Cause of the drag rise

From about Ma 0.7–0.8 (depending on the profile) local supersonic velocities occur. Drag rises disproportionately because of:

  • Wave drag (shock losses)
  • Shock–boundary layer interference → separation of the boundary layer from the surface
  • Friction drag due to the high velocity

In addition, the lift lost through the separated boundary layer in the rear region (unfavourable pressure distribution) has to be compensated by a larger angle of attack — which again costs drag.

Above Ma 1 the flow around the aerofoil is fully supersonic, the shocks ahead of the leading edge and at the trailing edge disturb the boundary layer less, and the drag coefficient drops again.

Supercritical aerofoil

The goal for subsonic aircraft is to shift the drag rise curve towards higher speeds. The supercritical aerofoil keeps the drag rise small through:

  • a weak shock
  • a far aft shock position
  • a uniform pressure distribution (“roof top”)

The same principle is applied to the external nacelle contour: supercritical velocities may occur well forward on the cowl as long as the local velocities are subsonic again well ahead of the maximum nacelle diameter. Cowls are often cambered in order to compensate for high angles of attack. NACA 1-series profiles are used.

Important distinction: the task of the wing is to generate lift — all other surfaces should ideally produce only small aerodynamic losses.

Name the general functional nacelle design requirements.

  • Cowling for engine and associated equipment in order to minimize aerodynamic losses and drag and to provide protection against FOD and environmental influences.
  • Housing of the accessories (gearbox with IDG, starter, pumps, pipes, control systems, oil/fuel/air lines, electrical cables) — and at the same time good maintenance access. The gearbox sits either below the fan (large volume required in the lower nacelle region) or below the core behind the fan.
  • Accommodation of thrust reverser, mixer and exhaust nozzle.
  • Attachment to the pylon.
  • Anti-icing installation (constrains the geometry of the intake lip).
  • Acoustic liner to meet the noise requirements (large area → long intake favourable).
  • Ventilation for accessory and engine cooling; build as small as possible, but cover all space demands.

Aerodynamic objectives

  • Intake flow; uniform pressure distribution over large parts of the profile (“roof top”), avoid supersonic velocities → no strong shocks and separations
  • Minimize surface and weight while providing sufficient and uniform air supply in all flight conditions with the lowest losses
  • Minimize aerodynamic interference with the airframe

Certification requirements in nacelle design

  • ETOPS requirements (drag while windmilling)
  • Ventilation against accumulation of dangerous flammable fluids and vapours
  • Fireproof fan cowl doors in the accessory zone; apron as fireproof barrier towards the pylon (firewall), fireproof firebox to protect the front mounts
  • Ventilation inlets for anti-icing, minimizing ingestion of hail and foreign objects

Geometric design constraints

  • Throat area (smallest area in the intake) — determines the mass flow
  • Intake lip radius ρ_N
  • Highlight radius / D₁ — determines the entry conditions; drag depends on the lip shape
  • Diffuser section up to the representative fan face area A_f
  • Fan radius (engine constraint)
  • Maximum nacelle radius D_max (arrangement of the accessories, above all AGB and IDG)
  • Nozzle radius (exit conditions, gas dynamics)

Characteristic ratios

  • Aspect ratio a/b

  • Overall contraction ratio = A_e/A₁ (throat area / highlight area) — with the smallest diameter in the intake and the contraction ratio the highlight area follows from it
  • Ratio D₁/D_max — at 0.85…1 and Ma > 0.85 the nacelle drag rises disproportionately

Further influences

External contour of the cowling, constraints from the airframe (e.g. ground clearance ≈ 0.5·D₁), aerodynamic interference with wing, fuselage and powerplant.

The intake in particular is a design challenge. Explain the different requirements influencing its aerodynamic contour.

Task of the intake: the fan shall receive the required amount of air in sufficient quality across the whole flight envelope. It is designed for the maximum fan mass flow, but at the same time for all off-design conditions.

The fundamental conflict of objectives

Cruise (design point)Take-off / climb / windmilling
Sharp, slender lip. It reduces the stagnation pressure at the highlight and the acceleration of the air passing outside. Necessary in order to avoid local supersonic regions, strong shocks and separation on the forebody. Blunt, rounded lip. The engine sucks from all directions, high velocities occur internally at the curvature; only a blunt lip prevents separation inside the intake.

The result is always a compromise that pays particular attention to avoiding severe engine instabilities.

The individual requirements

  • Mass flow in all flight conditions — throat area and contraction ratio
  • Angle of attack: upper lip supercritical (roof-top profile), lower lip “blended”/blunt because of the high local angles of attack during take-off and climb
  • Crosswind — wind speeds and directions defined in the CRS for ground run, take-off and starting
  • Limit distortion and swirl — key figure DC60, avoidance of compressor stall and surge
  • Maximize intake pressure recovery (pressure loss = thrust loss) while keeping drag low: pre-entry drag, bleed drag, cowl drag
  • Stanhope correlation — lip radius, highlight diameter and intake length determine the critical area ratio
  • Pre-compression of the incoming flow (Mach number dependent)
  • Protection against the environment: ground vortices, FOD, bird strike, hail, icing — anti-icing pipes in the lip constrain the contour; the stagnation point zone at the lip is the most critical part of the cowl (ice reduces the area and can hit the fan when it breaks off)
  • Acoustics: acoustic liner between anti-icing and fan casing → large area and a long intake are desirable
  • Minimize interference with wing/fuselage; intake shielding through a favourable position in the flow field
  • Off-design and OEI — the design conditions are normal operation (without thrust loss) and critical conditions (OEI, stall)
Usual configuration: subsonic pitot intake — low losses up to Ma 0.9; above and below that (e.g. ground operation) relatively high losses. The nacelle is additionally installed with a droop angle matched to the cruise attitude.

Stanhope correlation: explain the parameters and the physical principle. What happens below and above the curve? What do ε, ρ_N and (A₀/A₁)crit mean?

Physical principle

The intake splits the incoming flow into internal flow (captured stream tube A₀) and external flow. For the ideal case A₀ = A₁ the pressure distribution on the stream tube produces no drag. If A₀ < A₁, air has to “spill” around the lip (spillage). The velocity around the lip rises and a suction region develops → cowl suction (a forward directed force). If A₀/A₁ drops further, the overspeed becomes so large that sonic velocity is reached locally or the flow separates. The corresponding area ratio is the critical one.

The Stanhope correlation establishes exactly this relationship: between the critical area ratio (A₀/A₁)crit and the basic geometric parameters of the intake.

The parameters

  • ρ_N — intake lip radius. A large radius distributes the turning over a longer distance → lower overspeed, separation only at a smaller A₀/A₁, i.e. a smaller (A₀/A₁)crit. The price: a thicker lip → more form/wave drag in cruise and more weight.
  • D₁ — highlight diameter (entry diameter).
  • D_max — maximum nacelle diameter. The ratio D₁/D_max describes the “thickness” of the cowl; large values (0.85…1) are slender but sensitive to shocks.
  • l_E + ρ_N — intake length. Long intakes have a longer surface with an unfavourable pressure gradient and separate already at small spillage; short intakes with stronger curvature keep the flow stable longer and tolerate more spillage. Disadvantage of short intakes: high curvature, less area for the acoustic liner.
  • ε = A₀/A₁ — flow ratio, i.e. the area ratio of the captured stream tube to the highlight area. ε ≫ 1 at take-off, ε ≈ 0.5…0.8 in cruise, ε < 0.6 in descent/windmilling.
  • (A₀/A₁)crit — critical area ratio: the value below which the flow can no longer follow the pressure gradient and either separates (low velocity) or forms a shock (high velocity); the intake starts to choke.

Below and above the curve

  • Below the curve (ε above the critical value): attached flow, cowl suction acts and largely compensates the pre-entry drag → spillage drag small and nearly constant. Safe operating range.
  • Above the curve (ε below the critical value or Mach number too high): local supersonic regions, shock, boundary layer separation. The cowl suction is lost, spillage drag rises steeply, buffeting (large unsteady forces from shock–boundary layer interaction) has to be expected. Inadmissible range.

Empirical formulation from exercise A

ρ_N = D₁²/4 + 0.1365 · D_max² · (1 − D₁/D_max)² − D₁/2 — to be solved iteratively, then D₁ = D_e + 2·ρ_N.

What are intake pressure recovery and DC60? What does stable engine operation depend on?

Intake pressure recovery

η = P_fan face / P_freestream

Efficiency of the diffusion in the intake, i.e. a measure of the total pressure loss due to friction and wave drag. Total pressure loss in the intake means a direct thrust loss. Losses arise from wall friction and turbulent mixing in case of separation — which in turn creates drag, so the design has to be balanced between pressure recovery and drag (pre-entry, bleed, cowl drag).

Distortion coefficient DC60

DC60 = (P₁ − P₆₀) / q

  • P₆₀ — lowest pressure value in any 60° sector
  • P₁ — mean value of the total pressures in the intake
  • q — dynamic pressure averaged over the intake area

The higher the DC60 value, the greater the danger of compressor surge. For all full power conditions the intake must deliver attached flow; if separation does occur, the disturbance must not reach into the core stream. Up to DC60 = −0.1 freedom from surge and flutter is generally considered assured.

Influences on operational stability

  1. Operating conditions — Mach number, Reynolds number, angle of attack and sideslip angle
  2. Insufficient intake design — size, shape, boundary layer control
  3. Engine sensitivity — margin between surge line and working line
  4. External influences

Total pressure fluctuations at the fan face can be steady or time dependent; the latter either spatially uniform (“buzz”) or non-uniform (“turbulence”). Swirl creates a total pressure and thus a thrust loss and, with long curved intakes (integrated engines), can persist up to the fan face → compressor stall.

Name pros and cons of separated and integrated exhaust nozzles as well as of short and long nacelles.

Separate vs. integrated nozzle

Separate thrust nozzle: the cold stream has its own nozzle, no mixing inside the engine, each nozzle can be optimized individually.

  • + short nacelle, lower weight, lower manufacturing and maintenance cost
  • − performance loss, because the mixing of core and fan stream is missing
  • − higher noise level because of the higher exit velocity, smaller area for the acoustic liner
  • − pylon/nozzle interaction particularly critical: the pylon accelerates the flow in the exit plane, a recompression shock can develop in the upper part of the nozzle jet → efficiency loss
  • − interference with the wing possible

Integrated / mixed thrust nozzle: bypass and core stream share a common nozzle.

  • + mixing improves performance (energy exchange between the hot primary and the cold secondary stream), lower noise level
  • + no additional drag from a core nozzle cowling
  • + room for a forced mixer (lobe type) → additional noise reduction and SFC improvement
  • − a minimum length is required for the mixing process; a long cowl can create a nacelle–wing interference problem
  • − the pressure loss from the mixing process (different pressures) has to be weighed against the performance gain
  • − higher weight and higher cost
Mixing is most effective at low BPR. As BPR increases, the share of the core stream drops, the gain shrinks, and the trade against additional drag and weight becomes unfavourable.

Short cowl vs. long cowl

  • Short cowl is lighter and offers less area for friction drag; external compression only, with spillage at the design point creating additional drag. Underwing it is closely coupled to the wing (SDSF: fan exit ahead of the wing leading edge).
  • Long cowl is nevertheless often preferred because it lowers the noise level; mostly internal compression, with spillage in the nacelle duct at off-design and low Mach number. For rear fuselage mounting the long cowl is driven by the distance fuselage–powerplant and by the channel flow in the high speed regime; if properly designed, the installation can even have negative installation drag at transonic speeds, because the blockage behind the wing lowers the peak Mach numbers and weakens the shocks and shifts them forward.

How is nacelle drag composed? Explain spillage drag, cowl suction and pre-entry drag.

The drag produced by the nacelle counts not as a thrust loss but as part of the aircraft drag.

D = D_friction + D_pressure + D_pre-entry

The contributions

  • Skin friction drag — viscous shear stresses on the surface exposed to the flow.
  • Forebody form drag — additional contribution from the changed pressure distribution over the forebody. Friction + form drag of the forebody = profile drag; it rises slowly as the area ratio decreases, because of the growing adverse pressure gradients.
  • Pre-entry drag — force on the free stream tube ahead of the intake:
    D_pre = (p_c − p_∞)·A_c + ρ_c·v_c²·A_c − ρ_∞·v_∞²·A_∞
    Positive in the sense of drag for ε < 1. It rises with Mach number and falls as ε increases. In subsonic flow it is associated with neither wave nor friction drag nor shock–boundary layer effects, and therefore is not really drag in the usual sense.
  • Static pressure loss — characteristic value for the flow quality of the intake; with a small lip radius the losses grow as the radius decreases.

Cowl suction

For ε < 1 the spillage from inside to outside increases the velocity around the lip and creates a suction region — a force directed forward (i.e. thrust, not drag). For ε > 1, in contrast, the acceleration produces drag.

Spillage drag

D_spill = D_pre + D₀ — the difference between the drag of the fully flowing nacelle (A∞ = A_c) and the pre-entry force.

  • At low Mach numbers nacelle and pre-entry drag largely compensate each other → spillage drag small and nearly constant.
  • From about Ma 0.9 the drag rise begins: local shocks and separation disturb the flow, the cowl suction is lost, spillage drag becomes large and buffeting is imminent.

Drag reduction

  • Reduce nacelle length and thereby wetted area
  • stabilize the laminar boundary layer
  • optimize the pressure distribution (supercritical cowl profiles, NACA 1-series)
Critical region according to Fig. 9-11: at D₁/D_max ≈ 0.85…1 and flight speeds above Ma 0.85 the nacelle drag rises disproportionately; a large part of it is intake drag.

Explain the boattail effect and the influence of the afterbody shape on drag.

Physics

Where the cylindrical cowl shape changes into the conical one, a fluid particle follows a convexly curved path. The centrifugal force points outwards and is balanced by a pressure gradient → suction region at the boattail shoulder → drag.

Close to the jet exit the displacement effect of the jet deflects the external flow onto a concavely curved path. The centrifugal force now points towards the afterbody cowl → overpressure region at the rear end → thrust.

Optimum for low afterbody drag: small expansion at the boattail shoulder, strong recompression at the jet exit.

Two competing effects

  • Deflection: most effective with expanding jets — it strengthens the concave curvature, increases recompression and static pressure → less drag.
  • Entrainment: acts in the opposite direction — the high exit velocities accelerate the boundary layer and the external flow in the afterbody region and lower the static pressure. On the other hand, entrainment stabilizes the boundary layer against separation.

Dependence on the pressure ratio

  1. Without acceleration: only a wake, hardly any recompression.
  2. Small jet pressure ratio (jet Ma < free stream): deflection and entrainment; afterbody drag lower than in case 1.
  3. Jet Ma > free stream: deflection grows further; first minimum of the afterbody drag when jet exit velocity equals free stream velocity. Afterwards afterbody drag rises again.
  4. A further increase hardly changes the pressure distribution.

Influence of the shape

  • A1 slender: no separation in subsonic flow, drag nearly constant → best behaviour
  • A2 short: separation in subsonic flow which spreads upstream with increasing Mach number and turns into shock-induced separation near sonic speed
  • A3 very short (radius only at the shoulder, then linear): separation already at low speed just behind the shoulder, complete separation

Conclusion: for minimum afterbody drag the cone has to be designed slender. In addition, the core cowl should have no curvature — all turning should take place upstream of the fan nozzle exit plane.

How is the engine protected against FOD, bird strike, hail and icing?

Foreign object damage (FOD)

During taxiing foreign objects can be ingested. A ground vortex may form that reaches from the stagnation point on the ground up to the intake and picks up even larger objects. Countermeasures:

  • Sufficient ground clearance — as a rule it is already sufficient; usual is about 0.5·D₁ intake lip ground clearance
  • Blowaway jet: compressor bleed air is blown out at the lower front side of the cowl as a high velocity layer and breaks up the vortex

Bird strike and hail

The fan cone and the duct leading to the compressor have to be shaped so that objects do not enter the core but are ejected through the fan duct. Ingestion of small ice particles and rain into the core changes the thermodynamic conditions of the cycle.

Anti-icing

Hot air pipes in the intake lips prevent ice formation. FAR 25: no ice may accumulate on the engine or the air intake that impairs operation or leads to a significant loss of performance. The most critical part is the stagnation point at the intake lip: ice reduces the entry area and can hit the fan or other components when it breaks off.

Further design means

  • Preliminary design tools and design rules from experience
  • CFD analysis
  • Wind tunnel tests: through flown nacelle (TFN), installation drag, position optimization, intake distortion

Describe the effect of the TRU on the retarding force during landing.

Basic principle

The flow through the final nozzle is blocked and deflected forward — this produces a retarding force. On turbofans this is done via cascades and blocker doors; most high BPR engines use the fan stream only. Reverse thrust acts independently of the wheel brakes and the aerodynamic speed brakes.

Effect over the landing roll

  • Reverse thrust is most effective at high speed — exactly where the wheel brakes are weakest because of the limited friction coefficient and the heat input. It reduces the kinetic energy the brakes have to absorb.
  • The deceleration is largest at the beginning of the roll and decreases towards standstill.
  • Below the cut-off speed (typically 60 kt, defined in the CRS) it has to be switched off, otherwise hot exhaust gases or swirled-up dust and foreign objects are ingested again (re-ingestion) — these change the thermodynamic conditions of the intake air and can damage the engine.
  • A low cut-off speed and high efficiency maximize the total braking effect and minimize the landing distance.

Benefit and price

  • + less wear of brakes and tyres (economic benefit), increased landing safety in case of brake failure or an icy runway, shorter required landing distance, rejected take-off manageable
  • − higher complexity, additional weight, performance loss, additional loads, manufacturing and maintenance effort of the exhaust system

The design case is the aborted take-off, because the loads are highest there.

Which aspects have to be considered in the design of the thrust reverser? Which types exist?

Requirements

  • Favourable aerodynamic integration with the least possible flow disturbance; smooth contour matched to the aerolines
  • No leakage in cruise
  • Avoidance of the ingestion of exhaust gas, swirled-up dust, dirt and particles
  • Influence on stability and controllability of the aircraft during the roll-out
  • Heat resistant materials where hot spots can occur
  • Mount design for the additional loads
  • Operation on the ground for deceleration and ground maintenance; operability without gas re-ingestion down to the defined speed, emergency operation down to standstill

Selection criteria for the type

  • Engine size and weight
  • required reverse thrust performance
  • cost and number of parts
  • aircraft configuration (underwing / rear fuselage)

Types

  • Cascade / blocker door reverser — standard on large turbofans with separate nozzles, since only the cold stream is blocked.
  • Pivot door reverser — more modern variant; doors block and deflect the bypass stream without cascades.
  • Bucket door reverser — common on smaller engines; the mixed nozzle requires the reversal of both streams, and that is easier downstream of the mixing plane.

Compliance and regulations

In the wind tunnel, force and moment, the cut-off speed and the effect of deployment on the structure are measured — necessary for the assessment of stability and controllability. Regulations: FAR 25.933 (reverser systems), FAR 25.934 (thrust reverser system test), FAR 33.97 (thrust reversers).

With rear fuselage mounting the upper reverser jets can cause fatigue problems on the empennage; impingement of the exhaust gases can excite natural modes of the structure and impair controllability during deceleration. On VHBR/UHBR engines the close coupling additionally affects reverser efficiency and the loads on the wing.

Which forces are transferred by front and rear mount, and what is the rated break point?

Loads on pylon and aircraft

  • Direct forces: weight and thrust
  • Aerodynamic forces on the nacelle
  • Derived forces: gyroscopic forces and moments of rotating parts

Underwing configuration

  • Front mount (fixed): X forces (thrust), Y forces (lateral), Z forces (vertical). It transfers the complete thrust.
  • Rear mount (sliding): Y forces, Z forces and the moment M from the rotating shafts. The sliding avoids stresses from thermal expansion.

Rear mounted configuration (RFN)

  • Front mount: all X, Y and Z forces as well as the rolling moment M
  • Rear mount: sliding bearing, transfers X and Z forces

Rated break point

For emergencies such as a landing with the gear not fully extended, or a ditching, the engine–pylon connection must fail before the pylon–wing connection breaks. This avoids damage to the integral tanks and escaping fuel. The break point is triggered by overload in:

  • Z direction (vertical, positive/negative)
  • Y direction (lateral, positive/negative)
  • X direction (rearward load)

Types of mount

  • Core mount: core engine attached at the rear mount and at the fan casing. The internal flow creates forces at the front and thrust at the rear → during take-off a bending moment is created on the engine.
  • Fancase mount: front and rear mount directly on the pylon. The further forward front attachment reduces the moment from the aerodynamic loads of the external flow; the core thrust is reacted through thrust struts, which reduces the bending moment.

The distance between the mounts defines the minimum length of the pylon; its thickness follows from mount size and installed diameters — above all the air ducts have the largest diameter.

Which aspects have to be considered in the pylon installation (UWN), and how are the installations arranged inside the pylon?

Aspects to consider

  • Landing gear length — 18 inches ground clearance of the nacelle when taxiing at maximum load
  • Weight and wetted area
  • Landing gear position and spray water cone of the nose gear
  • Wing sweep and dihedral
  • Interference drag
  • Maintainability and accessibility of the cargo doors
  • Aeroelasticity of the wing (flutter)

Why the pylon is so long

The far forward installed position follows from the turbine burst trajectory: the wing and hence the fuel tanks have to be out of the danger zone. This results in the typical elongated pylon shape — and incidentally places the TRU well ahead of the wing.

Zoning inside the pylon

For safety reasons the lines have to be routed separately. The installation duct is divided into zones:

  • Upper part, three zones: flammable fluids — hot air ducts in the middle — electrics. The air ducts therefore separate flammable fluids from the electrics.
  • Lower part: hydraulics and fuel as well as low temperature air ducts and special electrical wiring.
  • The partitions between the zones act as firewalls; in addition an extinguishing system is integrated (Freon bottles).

The real difficulty is fitting all of this into the slender pylon: fuel and pneumatic lines, engine and reverser control, electrical cables and numerous instrumentation lines have to be packed tightly and still allow maintenance access.

Which aerodynamic effects arise between pylon, nacelle and wing?

Pylon for RFN

It lies in a largely invariable flow field. The cross section is aerofoil-like with a round leading edge and smooth curvature, designed for minimum drag. The incoming flow is bent slightly upwards by the downwash of the wing — the pylon geometry is adapted to this. Since the pylon shall not generate lift, a supersonic region develops on the upper and the lower side; the largest one lies near the nacelle–pylon junction — comparable to the wing–fuselage junction, where a fairing helps. It should be short (little surface drag) but long enough to keep the relative thickness limited for a given absolute thickness (pipes, structure).

Pylon for UWN

  • The further forward the nacelle, the lower the interference — but the longer the pylon and the disproportionately higher the pylon and installation weight. The pylon has to react the moment of the engine mass located far ahead of the pylon–wing attachment. In addition, the far forward mass increases the flutter tendency.
  • The exhaust jet impinges on the pylon → thrust loss and drag increase. The pylon creates two irregularities in the jet; on the upper side the wing flow creates a denser field which is influenced by the upward expanding jet → local lift loss.
  • If the pylon overlaps the wing leading edge, the shock on the upper wing surface moves forward: the pylon locally increases the amount of air flowing over the upper surface, and the geometric change of the leading edge at the pylon–wing junction raises the pressure locally.
  • Lateral bulges can reduce the front suction peak on the lower wing surface. Suction peaks occur particularly inboard of the nacelle on the lower wing surface — minimizing them is a main objective. A horizontal shift reduces the critical suction peaks more than a vertical one.
  • If the pylon leading edge overlaps, the high lift devices have to be adapted as well; in the gap between pylon and flap turbulent flow can develop that causes lift loss and drag.
  • A separate jets configuration exposes the pylon to the fast fan efflux → additional pylon surface drag.
Design rule: the pylon leading edge shall undercut the wing leading edge, i.e. lie behind the stagnation point of the wing leading edge.

Sketch the influence of the spanwise (lateral) nacelle position on flutter speed, wing bending moment, landing gear length and induced drag — and explain the trends.

Flutter speed

Determined by the engine centre of gravity and by the stiffness of pylon and wing. In principle, a shift towards the wing tip amplifies the aeroelastic effects, and a shift forward increases the flutter tendency. The pylon must be stiff enough, but its natural frequency must lie far away from that of the wing. Important for the exam: the effect of the spanwise position on flutter cannot be generalized and has to be determined individually for each aircraft.

Wing (root) bending moment / wing weight

The engine mass acts against the lift and relieves the root bending moment — the wing can be built lighter. The optimum lies theoretically at about 60–70 % of the semi span: beyond that the downward bending moment during taxiing and landing dominates, and the structural weight rises again. Right at the tip the lift distribution would be least disturbed, but the OEI yawing moment would force an excessively large vertical tail.

Landing gear length

With positive dihedral the wing rises outboard — the further outboard the nacelle, the greater the ground clearance and the shorter and lighter the landing gear may be. With four engines, however, the outboard engine determines the landing gear length via the 5° roll angle requirement.

Induced drag

The clean lift distribution has its maximum at about 0.40–0.45 of the semi span. A nacelle there disturbs it most. Overall: engines further outboard increase the induced drag, a position further inboard reduces it — the extreme case at the wing tip would be favourable again (winglet effect), but fails because of the OEI moment.

What else depends on the spanwise position

  • Interference drag with the fuselage — decreases further outboard; typical range B/D_max = 1.1…1.4, below which it rises steeply
  • Critical Mach number of the aircraft
  • Size and weight of the vertical tail for the OEI case — further outboard means a larger lever arm and a larger tail
  • With four engines in addition: flap size (outboard engine directly outside the flap), interference between the two nacelles, rotor burst distances
Remember the conflicting objectives: outboard is wanted by structural weight, ground clearance/landing gear and fuselage interference — inboard is wanted by induced drag, OEI yawing moment and flutter behaviour.

Name at least two advantages and two disadvantages of a rear fuselage nacelle installation (RFN). How does it affect the wing aerodynamics?

Advantages

  • Aerodynamically clean wing — free of pylon and nacelle. This improves above all the low speed performance (take-off, landing) and allows the whole span to be used for control surfaces and high lift devices.
  • More stable inflow to the intake over the flight conditions because of the proximity to the fuselage; smaller range of angles of attack at the nacelle.
  • Lower noise emission towards the ground and partial shielding of the cabin noise by the nacelles; higher safety in case of fire.
  • Shorter, lighter landing gear and a more favourable fuselage shape in this area; important when the nacelle diameter would be too large for underwing mounting (business jets).
  • Smaller OEI yawing moment, because the lever arm to the longitudinal axis is small.

Disadvantages

  • Missing relief of the lift loads in the wing → the wing structural weight rises by 2–4 %.
  • Higher bending moments in the fuselage between engine attachment and wing root (including the support frame) → higher fuselage structural weight.
  • Heavy masses far behind the centre of gravity: the overall CG is very sensitive to the loading condition; during loading it moves forward, and the fully loaded aircraft is harder to rotate. Compared with UWN the CG travel is 3–5 % MAC larger and the lever arm to the tail shorter → a larger horizontal tail and elevator are required, usually as a T-tail (hot exhaust gases). The T-tail brings problems at high angles of attack (deep stall) — high angles of attack are only permitted above a minimum speed.
  • Restricted accessibility for maintenance because of the proximity to the fuselage.
  • TRU exhaust jet at the empennage — fatigue problems, reduced controllability during roll-out.
  • Gully effect: nacelles and fuselage form a channel with convergent–divergent boundaries → shocks and drag rise. The structure between engine and cabin has to be reinforced (fragments, noise); spray water from the landing gear can reach the engine and requires shielding.
  • Shorter usable cabin length, since the support frame lies behind the cabin.

Effect on the wing aerodynamics

  • The wing stays clean — no pylon/nacelle interference, no slat cut-outs, no flaps exposed to hot exhaust gases.
  • The area increase of the stream tube ahead of the intake creates a pressure rise that shifts the shock position on the wing. At high transonic speeds this can be used deliberately: the shocks become weaker and move forward, and the critical Mach number of the aircraft rises. The strongest shocks of the clean wing lie at about 0.75–0.8 of the local chord; if the intake area is placed there, the effect is largest — and the wing shields the intake against FOD.
  • Conversely, the downwash of the wing changes magnitude and direction of the inflow to the engine and increases the distortion in this region.
  • Optimum vertical position typically 0.3 < h/D_max < 0.5; nacelle–fuselage distance about 0.25–0.3·D_max; intake lip at least the fuselage boundary layer thickness away (about 2 % of the fuselage length ahead of the intake).

Name the reasons for an underwing installation (UWN) as well as its disadvantages.

Reasons for UWN

  • Maintenance access — the most important reason for transport aircraft
  • Lift load compensation — the engine mass acts where lift is generated and reduces the root bending moment → lighter wing structure
  • Favourable aircraft CG — the CG of the empty aircraft lies ahead of that of the loaded one, longer tail lever arm, larger permissible CG travel without a weight penalty on the tail
  • Short installations, e.g. length of the fuel lines (tank in the wing)
  • Cabin noise is shielded by the wing
  • Undisturbed inflow to the engine
  • The nacelles can be used to house the landing gear

Disadvantages

  • Intake close to the ground → increased erosion and damage from dust, sand and foreign objects (FOD), ground vortices
  • Minimum ground clearance forces a longer and heavier landing gear — increasingly critical as fan diameters grow
  • Increased wing drag and reduction of the sweep effect; interference between nacelle, pylon and wing
  • Ground noise stronger than with RFN
  • Hot exhaust gases impinge on the flaps — remedies: a “jet gate” (no flap in the jet region, a high speed aileron instead — but this lowers C_Lmax) or titanium in the impinged area (more expensive, more drag and vibration)
  • Flutter tendency due to masses far forward

Why the nacelle belongs far ahead of the leading edge — and why not

Advantages far forwardDisadvantages far forward
  • Considerably lower interference: a forward shift reduces the interference noticeably (vertical shifts have only a small effect)
  • Required by the rotor burst trajectory — wing and tanks out of the danger zone; the TRU sits well ahead of the wing
  • At the same gully depth the nacelle may sit higher → more ground clearance, shorter landing gear
  • Undisturbed inflow, lower effective angle of attack, the shock moves downstream
  • Increases the flutter tendency — requires more torsional stiffness and therefore weight
  • Longer pylon → disproportionately higher pylon and installation weight, larger moment
  • Larger wetted area, more friction drag
  • With engines mounted far forward a clear thrust dependence of the drag appears
Typical values: SDSF nacelles x ∈ [−0.2c; 0], h ∈ [−0.1c; −0.05c] — e.g. x = −0.1c, h = −0.1c. LDMF nacelles x ∈ [0.15c; 0.3c]. A320: h = −0.16c (low position so that the flap can be retained).

The BPR of new engines keeps increasing. Discuss the effects of the BPR on the SFC (for UWN). Name pros and cons of high bypass ratios.

The basic effect

A higher bypass ratio accelerates a larger air mass less strongly. The jet velocity drops, propulsive efficiency rises and SFC falls — at the same time noise decreases. VHBR ≈ 10, UHBR up to 15 and more.

What limits the gain (installation losses)

  • Nacelle drag and strongly growing wetted area (larger frontal area per unit of thrust)
  • Losses at bypass and core nozzle
  • Intake and bypass duct losses
  • Interference drag due to the close coupling to the wing; a lift loss proportional to engine size that has to be compensated by a higher wing incidence — i.e. more drag
  • Weight, ground clearance and landing gear length

Therefore the installed SFC curve has a minimum: the optimum of BPR and fan pressure ratio for minimum fuel consumption lies at about BPR ≈ 8. At BPR = 15–20 the limit is reached — the BPR effect turns into an SFC penalty.

Two development strategies

  • A maximum of BPR ≈ 9 in order to keep the conventional turbofan design
  • A new engine concept for BPR up to 15 with all the development risk and questions of customer acceptance

Further pros and cons

  • + excess thrust usable in the low speed regime: reduced take-off thrust for less airport noise, shorter take-off distance, higher rate of climb (more distance to the ground → less noise), lower DOC through lower fuel burn → attractive for medium and long range
  • − UHBR considerably increases the local lift loss through the close coupling of bypass jet and wing; slat cut-outs cause problems with maximum lift and stall control; reverser efficiency and the aerodynamic loads on the wing are impaired
  • − as the nacelle diameter grows, the effective angle of attack drops and the shock moves upstream; the total lift loss is largest for UHBR, followed by VHBR and turbofan
  • Remedy: short duct or ¾ duct nacelles with the fan exit ahead of the wing leading edge but vertically close to the wing — this decouples the lower wing surface from the upper nacelle surface, but moves the interference problem to the upper wing surface

Compare overwing and underwing installation.

Motives for overwing mounting

  1. Less ground noise during take-off and climb through shielding by the wing
  2. Shorter landing gear
  3. Lower FOD risk on unpaved runways
  4. For STOL designs the Coandă effect can be used for high lift at low speed

Disadvantages of overwing mounting

  1. Disturbance of the aerodynamics of the upper wing surface → local lift loss and increased induced drag; transonic effects on the upper surface
  2. Interference drag of up to 10 % of the aircraft drag — even after careful contouring of nacelle and pylon
  3. The nacelle effectively acts as a low aspect ratio wing ahead of the wing: the load on the front part of the nacelle and the shed vortices create a large lift-dependent drag — practically negligible for underwing mounting
  4. Above the upper surface large pressure gradients arise from expansion and compression waves in the jet → possible structural problems; the maximum lies shortly downstream of the nozzle exit
  5. Poor accessibility for maintenance, disadvantages for safety and passenger comfort, higher cabin noise
  6. Nacelle, pylon, wing and fuselage form an inboard channel with acceleration/deceleration and possible transonic regions; nose down pitching moment

Underwing in contrast

  • + maintenance access, relief of the wing bending moment, lower cabin noise, undisturbed inflow, favourable centre of gravity
  • − ground clearance and FOD, interference drag of pylon and nacelle, additional pylon weight, ground noise, flap impingement

Examples of realized overwing concepts: VFW 614 and Beriev Be-200. Because of the aerodynamic disadvantages the configuration did not prevail; the drag penalty can, however, be offset against the saving in landing gear weight.

Which requirements does the rotor burst case impose on the integration?

FAR requirements for the rotor burst case

  • Of every vital system at least one must remain operational after the event
  • The risk of an uncontrollable fire must be minimized
  • On four-engine aircraft “engine-to-engine damage” must be avoided
  • After the event, controlled continued flight and a safe landing must be possible

Spread angles

  • ±15° — general fragment spread range. Critical control cables, wiring, fuel-carrying components and lines, vent and hydraulic lines as well as pneumatic ducts should preferably lie outside. Inside, they have to be duplicated and separated or protected by structure or shielding material.
  • ±5° — range of the 1/3 disk and intermediate fragments. For tanks above the engine in this range, protection against fuel leakage has to be provided: dry bays and shielding. The dry bay is sized on the basis of possible fragment trajectories through the tank wall and the resulting leakage, so that no fuel migrates to the engine or to other ignition sources; a dripping distance of at least 25.4 cm (10 inches) from potential ignition sources of the nacelle in the static case is regarded as acceptable.
  • With four engines the engines on one wing must lie outside each other's ±5° spread ranges of the intermediate fragments of fan and last turbine disk; if necessary a safe zone in the fuselage has to be provided for routing the systems.

Basis of the consideration: the fragments are assumed to have infinite kinetic energy — they would penetrate every part on their way. In addition, the region 5° ahead of and behind the plane of rotation counts as a danger zone. This is also the reason for the far forward installed position and the long pylon of a UWN.

Compare tractor and pusher propeller configuration. Explain in particular the effect on pitch (and yaw) stability with the physical reasoning.

The physical reasoning

If the inflow changes (Δα in pitch or Δβ in yaw), the propeller disk is approached at an angle. The blades therefore see different angles of attack around the revolution, and a resulting normal force N in the propeller plane develops which points in the direction of the disturbance.

  • If the propeller plane lies ahead of the centre of gravity (tractor), N creates a moment that amplifies the disturbance → destabilizing in pitch and yaw.
  • If it lies behind the centre of gravity (pusher), N acts against the disturbance → stabilizing.

Tractor

+ clean inflow → better propeller efficiency and less external noise · engine mass ahead of the CG → longer lever arm to the tail → smaller, lighter empennage · the propeller slipstream improves engine cooling · wing and tail surfaces lie in the slipstream → more lift and more control effectiveness at low speed (take-off, landing, low speed manoeuvres).

− higher cabin noise (propeller at the front) · the faster slipstream over the aircraft surface increases the friction drag (“scrubbing drag”, which can also be interpreted as a loss of propeller efficiency), proportional to the washed area · destabilizing in pitch and yaw · the pilot's view is partly obstructed.

Pusher

+ lower cabin noise and better pilot view · if the propeller sits directly behind wing or tail, it accelerates and re-energizes the flow → higher effectiveness at low speed and protection against separation or stall (weaker than with the tractor) · when fuselage mounted, the wetted area can be reduced by shortening the fuselage: the propeller removes the boattail drag and allows a much steeper fuselage closure without separation · stabilizing in pitch and yaw.

− higher external noise and lower efficiency, because the inflow is disturbed · the CG moves aft → shorter tail lever arm → larger, heavier empennage · engine cooling more difficult (propeller behind the engine) · with fuselage mounting a longer landing gear is required, because the propeller comes closer to the ground during rotation; in addition stones thrown up by the landing gear can damage the propeller · pieces of ice from wing or fuselage can hit the propeller.

The tractor is the usual choice for almost every type of aircraft; the pusher is gaining importance on light and ultralight sport and home-built aircraft. For seaplanes and amphibians, high mounted arrangements are favourable.

Name advantages and disadvantages of the tractor propeller wing integration (TPW) and the particular challenges of propeller integration.

Why propeller integration is harder than for jet engines

  • The open rotor is a strong source of noise and vibration. The structure near the blades is loaded with fluctuating pressure waves and has to be locally reinforced (sonic fatigue); for passenger comfort considerable insulation material is needed in the fuselage → weight.
  • Aerodynamically different: the nacelle of a turboprop is smaller than a jet engine nacelle, but the propeller diameter is considerably larger. The slipstream is extensive, fast and strongly swirled — interference effects are harder to predict and to remove.

TPW — advantages

  • Acceptable external noise
  • Good propeller efficiency
  • Good engine cooling
  • Favourable aircraft centre of gravity
  • Good maintenance access
  • Suitable for STOL aircraft — the energy of the fast efflux can be used by the flaps for additional lift

TPW — disadvantages

  • Greater landing gear height
  • Increased interference drag at the wing
  • Lower achievable Mach number than with a clean wing
  • High cabin noise

Swirl recovery

Part of the energy transferred by the propeller is contained in the swirl and reduces the axial velocity behind the propeller, i.e. the thrust. The wing acts like a stator and removes part of the swirl; the energy is converted back into additional axial velocity (thrust recovery). By adapting wing and nacelle geometry the effect can be strengthened.

Interference and remedies

Because of the swirl the wing experiences upwash on one side of the propeller and downwash on the other. The upwash increases the effective local angle of attack → separation and shock formation become more likely → the critical Mach number drops. Remedies:

  • LEX (leading edge extensions) to change the wing geometry locally
  • Contouring of the nacelle to align it with the local flow
  • Suitable droop and toe-in angles of the propeller (usually sufficient for turboprops below Ma 0.7)
  • Low propeller position: in principle lower positions produce less interference drag. On high wing aircraft the thrust line runs tangentially to the lower wing surface; as a compromise it may also lie tangentially to the upper surface (h_o = 0). Higher positions lead to inadmissible interference.
  • The gearbox allows an offset between propeller and engine centre line as well as separate droop angles for propeller and nacelle — so the vertical position can be adjusted without a pylon.

Which clearances and ground clearances do FAR 23/25 prescribe for the propeller integration?

Minimum clearances to the structure

  • 2.54 cm (1 inch) radial clearance between propeller and aircraft structure, plus any further clearance needed to avoid harmful vibration
  • 1.27 cm (0.5 inch) longitudinal clearance
  • positive clearance between other rotating parts of propeller or spinner and stationary structural parts

These are absolute minima. According to Torenbeek, in order to limit cabin noise and acoustic fatigue, at least 10 cm (4 inches) between propeller tip and fuselage plus 1.65 cm (0.65 inch) per 100 hp of engine power are advisable.

Prohibited zone in the propeller plane

Members of the minimum crew and parts of the cockpit or primary controls (except cables and control rods) must not lie in the region 5° ahead of and behind the plane of rotation of an inboard propeller. Cabin doors should not be too close either; passenger seats, cargo compartments and lavatories should also be avoided there. The fuselage has to be locally reinforced because pieces of ice can be shed from the propeller.

Ground clearance

  • 17.8 cm (7 inches) with nose wheel gear, 23 cm (9 inches) with tail wheel gear — with the gear statically compressed in take-off or taxi attitude, whichever is more critical
  • These values are practically only used by light piston engine aircraft. Turboprops have more ground clearance: commuters with piston engines 25–35 cm, with turboprops often 40 cm and more, larger types 45–65 cm.
  • According to a NASA propfan study the recommended ground clearance is proportional to aircraft weight and cruise Mach number divided by the square of the propeller diameter (suction force ≈ disk loading).

Further rules

  • The OEI requirement applies to propellers as well: the vertical tail must compensate the yawing moment of the remaining engine.
  • As with jet engines, wing mounting relieves the bending moment; on the other hand the propeller slipstream loads the structure → reinforcement required. For propfans this weight penalty can even exceed the relief.
  • Longitudinal position: sufficient distance between wing leading edge and propeller plane prevents interaction of the flow fields, reduces noise and vibration in wing and blade and preserves propeller efficiency.

Name the main geometric baselines for an engine in the CRS as well as the operational requirements.

The Airframer defines the engine position and the pylon interface. The corresponding data are given as the geometric baseline in the CRS:

Geometric baseline

  • Horizontal, vertical and lateral position relative to fuselage or wing — decisive for pylon design and aerodynamic interference
  • Engine attitude: toe-in / toe-out and droop angle
  • Rough geometry and position of engine mounts and thrust struts, so that enough space is provided in the powerplant design
  • Nacelle weight requirements (DOC, load on the mounts)

  • Flight envelope: altitude / Mach number / temperature
  • Engine operating attitude envelope, ground and crosswind requirements as well as other environmental conditions
  • Ground and in-flight relight and starting envelope
  • Installed performance assumptions and installed performance guarantees (thrust, SFC)
  • Reverse thrust
  • Maintenance related items: commonality and interchangeability between left and right engine; removal of powerplant or dressed engine with access to mounts and GSE attachment points; access for the inspection of fan blades and oil levels

Describe the aerodynamic integration design process and the preliminary installation design loop.

Process across the stages

  • Stage 0/1: assessment of engine cycles, fan sizes and nacelle concepts in studies, in order to prepare offers and to fix the external dimensions.
  • Stage 2: engine design and nacelle concept are frozen at stage 2 exit. The complete nacelle surface definition is achieved; verification by CFD and/or wind tunnel.
  • Stage 3: leads to engine certification and production start; the complete 3D design of the external and internal nacelle surfaces is finalized.
  • Stage 4: leads to aircraft certification, with close involvement of flight performance (installed drag), intake performance and reverser operation.

Preliminary nacelle design (stage 1) — the loop

  1. The RFP of the Airframer comes in at stage 1 entry.
  2. From the performance requirements the thermodynamic cycle and from it the core engine are designed.
  3. With the preliminary design of the core, the 5 main interface positions / physical hardpoints are fixed — the absolute minimum from which a large part of the nacelle geometry follows.
  4. Installations are fitted: the accessory gearbox on the IMC is one of the largest installations; the main reference point for the nacelle diameter is usually the IDG on the AGB. Larger generators push the aerolines outwards → more drag and weight. For more ground clearance the AGB can be mounted at the side (CFM56: A320 at the bottom, B737 at the side).
  5. Further inputs: bleed air off-takes create drag in the bypass stream; surface coolers for the oil sit in the rear bypass duct; additional electrical units to be cooled create pressure losses.
  6. Keep-out-zones for the nacelle reserve space in the powerplant DMU for geometry changes (1–2 %), skin thickness and the clearance demanded by the customer (usually about 10–20 mm).
  7. Numerous inputs, variables and dependencies make the procedure cyclic — the installation is broken down into mutually influencing components and iterated.
Important: already at stage 1 exit the nacelle geometry has a high accuracy — in stage 2 it only changes by 1–2 %. A problem is that installation engineering is only involved on demand in this early phase, which can lead to installation difficulties later.

Design tools

  • Preliminary design tools and design rules (experience)
  • CFD analysis
  • Wind tunnel: through flown nacelle, installation drag measurement, position optimization, intake distortion

What is the Propulsion System Specification, and which topics does it contain?

The internal specification document derived from the CRS, the certification requirements, lessons learnt and the company design standards, which serves as the reference for the whole design. It describes the complete propulsion system, not just the bare engine.

Typical content

  • Performance: thrust requirements over the flight envelope, SFC guarantees, ratings, reverse thrust
  • Geometry: dimensions, weight, centre of gravity, attachment points, baseline according to Fig. 7-1
  • Nacelle: aerolines, inlet cowl, fan cowl doors, fixed cowl, TRU, nozzle and mixer — laid down in the Nacelle Technical Requirements Document (technical design, performance, compliance, test and certification requirements); it is passed on to subcontractors who build parts of the nacelle
  • Systems and interfaces: air, fuel and oil system, control (FADEC/EEC), bleeds and power off-takes, EBU, BFE, drains and vents
  • Operation: envelopes, relight/starting, crosswind, environmental conditions
  • Maintainability: LRU list, accessibility, KOZ, MTTR/MMH targets, demonstrations
  • Certification: applicable CS/FAR, compliance plans, tests
  • Reliability and safety: IFSD rate, ETOPS capability, fire protection, burst considerations
Note the split of responsibility: the Engine Supplier designs the internal engine flow and all associated aerodynamic functions up to the exhaust nozzle. The mechanical TRU design usually lies with the Airframer or a subcontractor, while the aerolines are defined by the Engine Supplier. On large transport aircraft the Airframer designs the nacelle (high saving potential through aerodynamics), on smaller business jets the Engine Supplier does (tight installation space, little aerodynamic freedom). The intake is generally the responsibility of the Airframer.

Name the three main functions of the empennage. What determines the forward and the aft centre of gravity limit?

Three functions of the empennage

Trim · stabilize · control.

On naturally stable aircraft the task of the horizontal tail is to generate the moments required for control and stabilization during manoeuvres. The need for sufficient moment is the governing quantity for the tail design.

Natural longitudinal stability

The property of the aircraft to return to the stable flight condition after a disturbance — either linearly or in oscillations with decreasing amplitude. The reference is the neutral point. With a varying CG position the lever arm changes; the stability limit depends on the lift ratio (geometry of wing and tail) and on the points of force application.

The two limits

  • Aft limit: defined by a minimum value of natural stability — a stability requirement. The basis is the moment balance about the centre of gravity (without drag influence).
  • Forward limit: defined by the ability to handle the critical event, i.e. the case with the highest required control moment — a controllability requirement, in particular to compensate the moments in case of engine failure.

A safety factor ensures that the stability limit is not reached in real flight. The required CG range Δx defines the minimum tail area: S_H/S = horizontal tail area / wing area.

Relation to the integration: the engine position shifts the centre of gravity and thereby the permissible loading range — with RFN the CG moves so far aft that a larger tail (usually a T-tail) becomes necessary; with UWN the longer tail lever arm allows a larger CG travel without a weight penalty.

Sketch an RFN installation and point out the drag sources that occur, together with the physics behind them.

What to draw

The rear fuselage in side and top view section, the nacelle on a short pylon, the T-tail, plus the gully zone between nacelle, pylon and fuselage.

Drag sources and their physics

SourcePhysics
Skin friction dragViscous shear stresses on all wetted surfaces: nacelle skin, pylon, core cowl. The wetted area is the governing quantity — which is why the pylon has to be kept as short as possible.
Pressure / form drag (forebody)Changed pressure distribution over the forebody at a deviating area ratio; it grows with increasingly adverse pressure gradients.
Pre-entry / spillage dragForce on the free stream tube ahead of the intake. For ε < 1 air has to spill around the lip; as long as the flow stays attached, cowl suction largely compensates it. Above the critical area ratio the cowl suction is lost and spillage drag rises steeply.
Wave dragAt high subsonic Mach numbers the flow becomes locally supersonic — on the cowl, at the pylon and above all in the gully. Shocks create inviscid drag and, together with the boundary layer, trigger separation. Critical are the locations of maximum relative thickness.
Interference drag (gully effect)Nacelles, pylons and fuselage form a channel with convergent–divergent boundaries. The acceleration in the channel leads to shocks and boundary layer separation, particularly in the pylon–nacelle corners. The suction peaks lie at the maximum thickness of the respective underlying profile; therefore nacelle and pylon must not have their maximum thickness at the same axial position. Remedies: fuselage contouring, sufficient distance (≈ 0.25–0.3·D_max), toe-out and droop angle of the nacelle.
Boattail dragAt the boattail shoulder the convex path curvature forces a suction region (drag); close to the jet exit the concave turning creates an overpressure region (thrust). Goal: slender afterbody, little expansion at the shoulder, strong recompression at the exit.
Jet/empennage interferenceThe exhaust jet can reach the empennage: structural fatigue, loss of controllability — particularly critical with the reverser. In addition the wing downwash changes magnitude and direction of the inflow and increases the distortion.
Induced dragSecondary for the nacelle itself (no noteworthy lift, chargeability by weight share), but the installation changes the lift distribution and therefore the induced drag of the aircraft.
Worth mentioning on the positive side: if properly designed, a rear fuselage installation can even have negative installation drag at high transonic speeds, because the blockage behind the wing lowers the peak Mach numbers and weakens the shocks and shifts them forward.

How do engine and wing or fuselage influence each other? Which general installation design rules apply?

Effect of the engine on the wing

  • The flow around the nacelle changes the flow conditions at wing and fuselage. Compared with the clean design the local lift distribution shifts — the deviation from the desired elliptical distribution increases the induced drag.
  • Jet entrainment (UWN) changes the local flow direction near the wing leading edge and can accelerate the flow into the supersonic regime. If the jet increases the pressure gradient in the rear region of the lower surface, the flow can separate → lift loss.
  • The lift loss has to be compensated by a larger angle of attack, which in turn increases the shock strength on the upper wing surface.
  • Swept wings have swept isobars in order to delay supercritical flow and shocks. Velocity changes caused by the jet are not uniform along the span and locally reduce the isobar sweep — together with the increased velocity on the lower surface at mid chord this can make supercritical flow and shocks appear early.

Effect of the wing on the engine

  • Downwash behind the wing accelerates the flow; on fuselage mounted engines it changes magnitude and direction of the inflow and increases the distortion.
  • If the distance is too small, shocks arise from the gully effect — noise, vibration and drag; the flow at the horizontal tail becomes more critical.
  • At small distances the design of the high lift devices becomes more complicated (slat cut-outs, jet gate).

Influencing parameters

Flight Mach number and altitude; geometrically: nozzle exit area, intake/nozzle throat, wing sweep, flaps, bleeds, bypass duct. Varying over the flight conditions: landing gear extended/retracted, flap setting, reverser operation, engine speed, angle of attack, ambient conditions.

General installation design rules

  • Avoid stagnation regions, vortex formation and premature cowl shock formation caused by the intersection angle of strut leading edge and cowl
  • Avoid lift on the nacelle and optimize its attitude; do not direct the jet onto wing or flaps
  • Toe-in of the nacelle to align it with the cross flow at the wing
  • The pylon leading edge shall undercut the wing leading edge (behind the stagnation point)
  • Minimize gully effects — Mach number in the channel not above 1.1
  • Consider the interferences between wing, fuselage, empennage, nacelle and pylon as a whole, together with the requirements from structure, flight mechanics and systems

What are continuous and what are discrete design parameters? What are design criteria?

This question is marked as uncertain in the question catalogue and is not literally covered in the script — presumably it comes from a lecture slide. The following answer is the sensible interpretation in the context of the lecture.

Continuous parameters

Quantities that can be varied steplessly and used as control variables in optimization loops:

  • Bypass ratio, overall pressure ratio, turbine entry temperature
  • Fan diameter, mass flow, thrust
  • Nacelle length and diameter, lip radius, contraction ratio
  • Position of the nacelle: x/c, h/c, 2y/b, toe-in and droop angle

Discrete parameters

Quantities that can only take integer or countable values and therefore represent a concept decision:

  • Number of engines (2, 3, 4) and of shafts
  • Number of compressor and turbine stages
  • Installation location: underwing / rear fuselage / overwing / integrated
  • Nozzle concept: separate or mixed; nacelle: short or long cowl
  • Reverser type: cascade, pivot door, bucket door
  • Choice of materials (e.g. Kevlar or titanium fan casing)

Design criteria

According to script Ch. 1.1.1 the governing design criteria are the number of seats or payload and the required average range; added to these are mandatory criteria such as evacuation requirements as well as frequently changing requirements on noise and emissions. They follow from the market demand and act on fuselage, wing, systems, externals and engine design.
